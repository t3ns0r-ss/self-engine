## 1. Why this topic exists

Here is a small problem. There are $n = 20$ workers and 20 jobs, and $c[i][j]$ is the cost of giving job $j$ to worker $i$. Give every worker exactly one job, as cheaply as possible.

Trying every assignment means trying every permutation of the jobs ([topic 0.5](../../phase-0/0-5-brute-force/)): $20! \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{18}$ of them. Greedy "each worker takes the cheapest free job" fails, because a cheap choice early can take the only good job of a later worker.

The key idea: assign the workers in order $0, 1, 2, \dots$. After the first $k$ workers have their jobs, the rest of the problem only depends on **which** jobs are taken, not on who took them. A set of jobs is a subset of 20 items, stored as the bits of an integer ([topic 1.7](../../phase-1/1-7-bits/)), so there are only $2^{20} \approx 10^6$ states. Each state tries at most 20 jobs for the next worker: $2 \cdot 10^7$ steps. This is **bitmask DP**: DP whose state is (or contains) a subset of a small set.

### What this topic does not cover

- Sums over all subsets of every mask in $O(2^n n)$ (the zeta transform, "sum over subsets"): not part of this course.
- DP over a grid row by row where the state is the shape of the boundary (profile DP, "broken profile"), and searches over (vertex, mask) states with a queue: they need graph search (topic 4.1) and are not covered here.
- Exact algorithms for much larger $n$: assignment with $n = 500$ is a matching problem, outside this course.

## 3. Definitions

- A **mask** is an integer read as a subset of $\{0, 1, \dots, n-1\}$: element $j$ is in the subset when bit $j$ is 1 (topic 0.5). Example: with $n = 4$, the mask $1011_2 = 11$ is the set $\{0, 1, 3\}$.
- The **size** of a mask is its number of 1 bits, `__builtin_popcount(mask)`.
- A **submask** of a mask $m$ is a mask $s$ with $s \subseteq m$, that is `(s & m) == s`. The submasks of $1011_2$ are $1011, 1010, 1001, 1000, 0011, 0010, 0001, 0000$.
- A **Hamiltonian path** visits every vertex of a graph exactly once. The **travelling salesman problem** (TSP) asks for the shortest closed route that visits every city exactly once and returns to the start.

## 4. Theory and proofs

### 4.1 Assigning items one at a time

**Theorem 3.7.1 (assignment over masks).** Let $\text{dp}[m]$ be the least cost of giving the first $|m|$ workers (workers $0..|m|-1$) the jobs in the set $m$, one job each. Then $\text{dp}[0] = 0$ and
$$\text{dp}[m] = \min_{j \in m} \left(\text{dp}[m \setminus \{j\}] + c[|m| - 1][j]\right).$$
The answer is $\text{dp}[2^n - 1]$. Filling the masks in increasing numeric order is an acyclic order. Time $O(2^n \cdot n)$, memory $O(2^n)$.

:::tip[In plain words]
When items are used at most once and the future depends only on which ones are used, store the set of used items as the bits of a number. The next item to place is given by how many are used, so the table has 2^n states instead of n! orders.
:::

**A tiny instance.** Trace for $c = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 0 & 5 \\ 3 & 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ (rows are workers, columns jobs; masks written as job 2, job 1, job 0):

| mask | 000 | 001 | 010 | 011 | 100 | 101 | 110 | 111 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| dp | 0 | 4 | 1 | 3 | 3 | 5 | 3 | 5 |

For example $\text{dp}[011]$: worker 1 takes job 0 after worker 0 took job 1 ($1 + 2$), or job 1 after job 0 ($4 + 0$); the minimum is 3. The answer is 5: worker 0 takes job 1, worker 1 job 0, worker 2 job 2.


**Proof.**
1. In an assignment of the jobs $m$ to the first $|m|$ workers, the last of these workers, number $|m| - 1$, has some job $j \in m$, and the others have exactly the jobs $m \setminus \{j\}$.
2. Their cost does not depend on which order they chose in, so the best for them is $\text{dp}[m \setminus \{j\}]$ (Theorem 3.2.1).
3. Removing a bit makes the integer smaller, so every state is computed after the states it reads (Theorem 3.2.3). $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Only the **set** of used items matters for the future, not the order in which they were used or by whom. Which worker comes next follows from the size of the set.
2. $n$ is small: $2^n \cdot n$ steps, so $n \le 20$ (or 23–25 with a light inner loop).
:::

### 4.2 Visiting every vertex

**Theorem 3.7.2 (paths through every vertex).** In a graph with $n$ vertices and edge lengths $d(v, u)$, let $\text{dp}[m][v]$ be the length of the shortest path that starts at vertex $s$, visits exactly the vertices of $m$, each once, and ends at $v \in m$. Then $\text{dp}[\{s\}][s] = 0$ and
$$\text{dp}[m][v] = \min_{u \in m \setminus \{v\}} \left(\text{dp}[m \setminus \{v\}][u] + d(u, v)\right).$$
The shortest Hamiltonian path from $s$ is $\min_v \text{dp}[\text{all}][v]$, and the shortest TSP tour is $\min_v (\text{dp}[\text{all}][v] + d(v, s))$. Time $O(2^n n^2)$, memory $O(2^n n)$. With $+$ in place of $\min$, the table counts the Hamiltonian paths.

:::tip[In plain words]
To visit every city exactly once, remember which cities are visited and where you are. The shortest path to a state comes from the shortest path to the state without the last city, plus one edge.
:::

**A tiny instance.** Four cities with distances $d$ (row = from, column = to) $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 & 9 & 10 \\ 1 & 0 & 6 & 4 \\ 15 & 7 & 0 & 8 \\ 6 & 3 & 12 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Starting from city 0, the table gives the tour $0 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1 \to 0$ of length $9 + 8 + 3 + 1 = 21$; trying all $3! = 6$ orders confirms it.


**Proof.**
1. A path that visits exactly $m$ and ends at $v$ has a vertex $u$ just before $v$ (if $m \ne \{s\}$); without its last edge it visits exactly $m \setminus \{v\}$ and ends at $u$.
2. Whether it can continue depends only on which vertices are used and where it stands, so the best path for $(m \setminus \{v\}, u)$ can be extended (Theorem 3.2.1).
3. For counting, the last vertex before $v$ splits the paths into disjoint groups (Theorem 3.5.1). $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The future depends only on the set of visited vertices and the current vertex, not on the order of the earlier visits.
2. $2^n n^2$ fits: $n \le 16$–18 for a minimum (about $10^8$ steps at $n = 18$), $n \le 20$ when many transitions can be skipped.
:::

### 4.3 Packing into groups

**Theorem 3.7.3 (fewest groups).** Items have weights $w_j \le C$. For a set $m$ of items, let $\text{best}[m]$ be the smallest pair (groups used, total weight in the last group), compared first by the number of groups, over all ways to pack $m$ into groups of total weight at most $C$, filled one after another. Then $\text{best}[0] = (1, 0)$ and
$$\text{best}[m] = \min_{j \in m} \text{add}(\text{best}[m \setminus \{j\}], w_j), \quad \text{add}((g, f), w) = \begin{cases} (g, f + w) & \text{if } f + w \le C,\\ (g + 1, w) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$
The fewest groups for all items is the first number of $\text{best}[\text{all}]$. Time $O(2^n n)$.

:::tip[In plain words]
To pack items into groups with a capacity, add the items one at a time and keep, for each set of packed items, the smallest pair of groups used and weight of the last group. A pair that is smaller is never worse for what comes next.
:::

**A tiny instance.** Weights $(4, 8, 6, 1)$ and capacity 10. The table gives $\text{best}[\text{all}] = (2, 9)$: two groups, for example $\{4, 6\}$ and then $\{8, 1\}$ with last fill 9.


**Proof.**
1. Any packing can be built by adding the items one at a time, finishing one group before starting the next; the last item added belongs to the last group.
2. So every packing of $m$ comes from a packing of $m \setminus \{j\}$ for some $j$ followed by adding $j$.
3. It remains to show that keeping only the smallest pair loses nothing: if $(g, f) \le (g', f')$, then $\text{add}((g, f), w) \le \text{add}((g', f'), w)$.
4. If $g = g'$ and $f \le f'$, the item fits in the first case whenever it fits in the second, and the results compare in the same order.
5. If $g < g'$, the first result has at most $g + 1 \le g'$ groups, and when it has exactly $g'$ groups its last group holds only $w$, at most the last group of the second result. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Groups are interchangeable and their order does not matter, so a packing can be built group by group.
2. For a pair of states with the same set, the one with fewer groups, or the same number and a lighter last group, is never worse for the future (the monotonicity shown in the proof).
:::

<TheoremCode unit="3.7/thm-3" />



### 4.4 All submasks

**Theorem 3.7.4 (submask enumeration).** For a mask $m$, the loop `for (s = m; s > 0; s = (s - 1) & m)` visits every non-empty submask of $m$ exactly once, in decreasing order. Running it for every mask $m$ of $n$ bits takes $3^n$ steps in total.

:::tip[In plain words]
The loop s = (s − 1) & m visits every submask of m exactly once, from large to small. Running it for every mask makes 3^n steps, because each item is either outside the mask, in the mask only, or in the submask.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The non-empty submasks of $1011_2 = 11$, in the order the loop visits them, are $11, 10, 9, 8, 3, 2, 1$.


**Proof.**
1. $(s - 1)$ turns the lowest 1 bit of $s$ into 0 and all bits below it into 1; `& m` keeps only the bits allowed by $m$.
2. The result is the largest submask of $m$ that is smaller than $s$: it agrees with $s$ above the lowest 1 bit of $s$, has a 0 there, and has every allowed bit set below.
3. So the loop steps through the submasks in decreasing order without skipping any, and stops after the smallest non-empty one.
4. For the total, a pair (mask $m$, submask $s$) chooses for each of the $n$ elements one of three options: not in $m$, in $m$ but not in $s$, in $s$. That gives $3^n$ pairs. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. $3^n$ fits: $3^{15} \approx 1.4 \cdot 10^7$, $3^{18} \approx 3.9 \cdot 10^8$; so $n \le 16$–18.
2. A typical use: $\text{dp}[m] = \max_{s \subseteq m,\ s \ne \emptyset} (\text{value}[s] + \text{dp}[m \setminus s])$, splitting the set into a first group and the rest. Requiring that $s$ contain the lowest element of $m$ counts each split into groups once.
:::

## 6. Templates

Four functions, one per card: assignment of jobs to workers, the shortest tour through all cities, the fewest groups under a capacity, and the best split of a set into groups by submasks. The brute forces try every permutation, or every partition into groups by recursion.

### Assignment over masks (Theorem 3.7.1)

<CodeSnippet unit="3.7/assignment" />

**How it works.** `dp[mask]` is the least cost for the first `popcount(mask)` workers to take exactly the jobs in `mask`. The loop runs over the masks in increasing order and pushes each finished value forward: the next worker, number `popcount(mask)`, takes any free job $j$. Every mask is final before it is read, because adding a bit makes the integer larger. Time $2^{20} \cdot 20 \approx 2 \cdot 10^7$.

**Alternative formulation.** "Pull" instead of "push": $\text{dp}[m] = \min_{j \in m} \text{dp}[m \setminus \{j\}] + c[|m| - 1][j]$, exactly the formula of Theorem 3.7.1. Both visit every (mask, job) pair once.

### Shortest tour (Theorem 3.7.2)

<CodeSnippet unit="3.7/tsp" />

**How it works.** `dp[mask][v]` is the shortest path from city 0 that visits exactly the cities of `mask` and stands at $v$. Only masks containing city 0 matter, so the loop steps by 2 (odd numbers have bit 0 set). From each reachable state, the path is extended to any unvisited city $u$. At the end, every full path is closed with the edge back to 0. Time $2^{16} \cdot 16^2 \approx 1.7 \cdot 10^7$.

**Alternative formulation.** Memoised recursion `go(mask, v)` = the shortest way to finish from $v$ when `mask` is visited: try every next city, end with the edge back to 0. It computes the same states backwards and is often shorter to write.

### Fewest groups (Theorem 3.7.3)

<CodeSnippet unit="3.7/fewest-groups" />

**How it works.** `best[mask]` is the smallest pair (groups, weight of the last group) over all ways to pack the items of `mask`, filling groups one after another. The last item packed is some $j$: it either fits into the last group of the best packing of the other items, or opens a new group. `std::pair` compares by the first value, then the second, which is exactly the order of Theorem 3.7.3. The answer is the group count for all items.

**Alternative formulation.** The submask method of the next template also works: $\text{groups}[m] = 1 + \min \text{groups}[m \setminus s]$ over submasks $s$ that fit in one group. It costs $3^n$ instead of $2^n n$, so the pair method is the one to use for $n = 20$.

### Best split into groups (Theorem 3.7.4)

<CodeSnippet unit="3.7/best-partition" />

**How it works.** `sum[mask]` is precomputed for all masks from the mask without its lowest bit. Then `dp[mask]` is the best score for splitting `mask` into groups: the group containing the lowest item of `mask` is some submask $s$, allowed if it fits the capacity, and the rest is split independently. Requiring the lowest item in $s$ makes each split counted once. All masks with all submasks take $3^{16} \approx 4.3 \cdot 10^7$ steps.

**Alternative formulation.** Without the lowest-item rule, also try every $s$; the result is the same (each split is just found several times), at a cost of roughly twice the work. The rule matters when counting splits instead of optimising.

## 7. Bug catalogue

Each bug below was made by changing a template from Section 6 and caught by `scripts/stress.sh`. The input shown is the shortest failing input among the first 2000 random seeds, or a hand-made input of the same size.

### Bug: masks in decreasing order

Template: assignment over masks.

```cpp
for (int mask = (1 << n) - 1; mask >= 0; mask--) {
```

Input: the example of Section 4.1 (`3`, then the matrix `4 1 3 / 2 0 5 / 3 2 2`).

Wrong output `9223372036854775807`, correct output `5`. The template pushes each finished value to larger masks; going downwards, every large mask is visited before any value has reached it, so nothing is ever extended beyond the empty set.

**Fix:** visit the masks in increasing order: a mask with a bit removed is a smaller integer, so it is always finished first (Theorem 3.7.1). Printing `LLONG_MAX` is a sign that a state was never reached.

### Bug: the tour is not closed

Template: shortest tour.

```cpp
for (int v = 1; v < n; v++) best = min(best, dp[(1 << n) - 1][v]);
```

Input: `2`, then the times `0 5 / 3 0`.

Wrong output `5`, correct output `8`. `dp[full][v]` is a Hamiltonian **path** ending at $v$; a tour must also come back to city 0.

**Fix:** add `d[v][0]` (Theorem 3.7.2). Read the statement twice: "visit every city" (a path) and "return to the start" (a tour) differ by exactly this term.

### Bug: keeping the first option with the fewest groups

Template: fewest groups.

```cpp
if (option.first < best[mask].first) best[mask] = option;
```

Input: `5 5`, then the weights `1 3 2 2 2`.

Wrong output `3`, correct output `2` ($\{1, 2, 2\}$ and $\{3, 2\}$). Among the packings with the fewest groups, the one with the lightest last group must be kept; keeping whichever came first can leave a fuller last group, and a later item then opens a group it did not need.

**Fix:** compare the whole pair, groups first and then the fill (Theorem 3.7.3); `std::pair` and `min` do exactly that.

### Bug: starting without an open group

Template: fewest groups.

```cpp
best[0] = {0, 0};
```

Input: `1 2`, then `1`.

Wrong output `0`, correct output `1`. With no group open, the first item still "fits" into the last group, which does not exist, and is never counted.

**Fix:** start with one empty open group, $(1, 0)$. Check the base case on a single item.

### Bug: stepping through all smaller numbers

Template: best split into groups.

```cpp
for (int s = mask; s > 0; s = s - 1) {
```

Input: `3 7`, then the weights `2 2 5`.

Wrong output `69`, correct output `53` ($\{2, 5\}$ and $\{2\}$: $49 + 4$). Without `& mask`, `s` runs through numbers that are not submasks; `mask ^ s` then contains items outside the mask, and the same item is counted in two groups.

**Fix:** `s = (s - 1) & mask` (Theorem 3.7.4). Whenever a loop is meant to visit subsets of a mask, check that every visited `s` satisfies `(s & mask) == s`.

## 8. Worked examples

### Example 1: Matching

[AtCoder Educational DP Contest (EDPC), problem O: Matching](https://atcoder.jp/contests/dp/tasks/dp_o). In short: $N \le 21$ men and $N$ women, with a 0/1 table of compatible pairs; count the ways to pair everyone with a compatible partner, modulo $10^9 + 7$.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$N = 21$: $2^{21} \approx 2 \cdot 10^6$ masks, times 21 choices, is $4.4 \cdot 10^7$ steps. $21! \approx 5 \cdot 10^{19}$ permutations are far too many.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Trying every permutation of the women (topic 0.5): $21!$.
- A formula (topic 2.3): the compatibility table is arbitrary, so no product or binomial rule applies.
- Counting over masks of used women (Theorem 3.7.1, with sums): the "Assigning items with a mask of used ones" card.

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The card needs that only the set of used items matters. Pair the men in order $0, 1, \dots$: when the first $k$ men are paired, which of them got which woman does not affect who can pair the rest; only the set of taken women does, and $k$ is its size. Decision: $\text{ways}[m] = $ the number of ways for the first $|m|$ men to take exactly the women in $m$, pushed forward over the free compatible women.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

A pairing of the first $|m|$ men with the women $m$ has a unique woman $j$ for the last man $|m| - 1$; removing that pair leaves a pairing of the first $|m| - 1$ men with $m \setminus \{j\}$, and different pairings stay different. So the pairings for $m$ split into disjoint groups by $j$, each of size $\text{ways}[m \setminus \{j\}]$ when the pair is compatible (Theorem 3.5.1 on the states of Theorem 3.7.1). Increasing mask order computes every state before it is read.

### Example 2: Special Permutations

[LeetCode 2741: Special Permutations](https://leetcode.com/problems/special-permutations/). In short: given $n \le 14$ distinct positive integers, count the orders in which every two neighbours divide one another, modulo $10^9 + 7$.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$n = 14$: $2^{14} \cdot 14^2 \approx 3.2 \cdot 10^6$ steps for a table over (set, last). $14! \approx 8.7 \cdot 10^{10}$ orders cannot be listed.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Trying every order (topic 0.5): $14!$.
- A table over the position and the last number only (topic 3.2): it cannot tell which numbers are still unused.
- Counting paths through every vertex (Theorem 3.7.2): the "Paths through every vertex" card.

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

Think of the numbers as vertices, with an edge between two numbers when one divides the other. A special permutation is a path that visits every vertex exactly once. Whether it can continue depends only on the set already used and the last number. Decision: $\text{ways}[m][v]$ = orders of exactly the numbers of $m$ that end with $v$, summed over the full set.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

An order of the set $m$ ending with $v$ has a unique number $u$ just before $v$ (if $|m| \ge 2$), and $u, v$ must divide one another; removing $v$ gives an order of $m \setminus \{v\}$ ending with $u$. So the orders split into disjoint groups by $u$, and the sum rule of Theorem 3.7.2 counts them. The base states are the single numbers, one order each. Each state is read only after all smaller masks are done.
