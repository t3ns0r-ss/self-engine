This topic combines everything before it: the 47 lessons of [Phase 0](../../phase-0/) to [Phase 7](../), and above all their Recognition Cards. It teaches no new algorithm. In a contest nobody tells you that a problem is "a sliding window" or "a tree DP"; the statement gives numbers and a question, and the first job is to find the tool. This lesson is a short guide to doing that job in a fixed order, a recap of the whole Recognition Handbook, and mixed sets in which the topic of every problem is hidden.

## 1. Why this topic exists

Here are two statements that look alike. In the first, an array of $n \le 2 \cdot 10^5$ numbers is given and you must count the subarrays whose sum is exactly $k$; in the second, the same with all numbers positive and the question is the *longest* such subarray. The first one is a prefix-sum problem with a map of counts (the numbers may be negative, so a window cannot be shrunk safely); the second one is a sliding window. The words "subarray" and "sum" are in both statements and they fix nothing. What differs is a property: *can the window be shrunk without losing a valid answer?* A beginner picks the tool by the words and is wrong half of the time; a solver who has the habit of checking the property is wrong rarely, and notices at once when they are.

This lesson trains the habit. Every problem of the course so far was given with its topic: the lesson told you which card to use, and the ladder problems all fit that card. Contests do not. Rating 2000 to 2400 problems also often *combine* two ideas, so a wrong first guess costs a long detour. The lesson has three parts:
- **A fixed routine.** Read the constraints, list the candidate tools, check the decisive property of each on the actual statement, and then look for the look-alike that needs something else (Section 5).
- **A recap of all cards.** One table for every phase, so that you can see in one place what the tools are (Section 5). The full version, with weak signals, is the [Recognition Handbook](../../handbook/).
- **Mixed sets.** Review sets and a checkpoint made of problems from all phases, with no topic named (Sections 10 to 13).

## 3. Definitions

- A **weak signal** is a phrase or a shape of the input that suggests a few tools and proves nothing ("longest segment", "count the pairs", $n \le 20$). Many problems share the wording and need different tools.
- A **decisive property** is the precondition on which the correctness proof of a tool stands. Example: for a sliding window, shrinking a valid window keeps it valid. The property, checked on the actual statement, decides.
- A **kill signal** is a feature of the statement that rules a card out, such as negative values for a window, or a huge index for a loop over the states.
- A **look-alike** is a problem whose statement resembles one of a card but that needs another tool. Each lesson has pairs of them.
- A **Recognition miss** is a first attempt with a tool that turns out wrong for the problem, even if the problem is solved later. The mixed sets ask you to count them.
- The **budget** of a problem is the number of simple steps the limits allow, about $10^8$ per second; from the largest bound you read the growth rate you can afford.

## 5. Recognition Cards

This section replaces the theory and the templates of the other lessons by the recap. Read the routine first, and then use the tables to refresh the tools.

### The routine

1. **Constraints → budget.** Write down the largest bound and the growth rate it allows. For $n \le 2 \cdot 10^5$ you can afford $O(n \log n)$; for $n \le 20$ a search over subsets; for $n \le 100$ with an index up to $10^{18}$ a matrix power; for $n \le 5000$ a quadratic DP.
2. **Question form → candidate tools.** Write two or three tools that the question suggests ("the number of ways" → a DP or a formula; "the smallest" → binary search, greedy or shortest path; "for every query" → a data structure). Add the brute force, always: if its cost is within the budget it is the answer.
3. **Property check → decision.** For each candidate, name the decisive property and test it on the statement, with the real limits. Reject on a kill signal. If two candidates survive, take the one with the simpler proof.
4. **Look-alike check.** Ask whether a small change of the statement would change the tool (negative values, a repeated item, a huge index, a count in place of an existence). If so, check that your statement is on the side you think.

Then write down three lines (the budget, the candidates, the property), solve, and log whether the first tool was right.

### The confusions that cost the most

These are the pairs of tools that statements make easy to mix up. Each lesson gave the test; here it is in one line.

- **Window or prefix sums** ([1.3](../../phase-1/1-3-two-pointers/), [1.2](../../phase-1/1-2-prefix-sums/)): can a valid window be shrunk and stay valid? Negative values say no.
- **Greedy or DP** ([1.5](../../phase-1/1-5-greedy-1/), [3.2](../../phase-3/3-2-dp-foundations/)): can you prove an exchange argument? If a counterexample on five items exists, the problem is a DP.
- **BFS or Dijkstra** ([4.1](../../phase-4/4-1-graph-traversal/), [4.3](../../phase-4/4-3-shortest-paths/)): are all edges of the same weight? Weights $0$ and $1$ need a deque.
- **Subset DP or search** ([3.1](../../phase-3/3-1-backtracking/), [3.7](../../phase-3/3-7-bitmask-dp/)): is $n$ at most about 20 and the question a count or an optimum over subsets?
- **Sparse table, Fenwick tree or segment tree** ([5.1](../../phase-5/5-1-sparse-table/), [5.2](../../phase-5/5-2-fenwick/), [5.3](../../phase-5/5-3-segment-tree/)): are there updates? Is the operation idempotent, invertible, or neither?
- **Hashing, Z-function, trie or suffix array** ([6.1](../../phase-6/6-1-string-hashing/), [6.2](../../phase-6/6-2-prefix-function/), [6.3](../../phase-6/6-3-trie/), [6.4](../../phase-6/6-4-suffix-array/)): are you comparing equal pieces anywhere, matching a few patterns, sharing the beginnings of many words, or ordering all suffixes?
- **A loop or a matrix power** ([3.2](../../phase-3/3-2-dp-foundations/), [7.6](../../phase-7/7-6-matrix-exponentiation/)): is the index up to $10^7$ (loop) or up to $10^{18}$ (matrix)?
- **Counting or constructing** ([3.5](../../phase-3/3-5-counting-dp/), [7.7](../../phase-7/7-7-constructive/)): is the answer a number of objects or one object?
- **A game DP or Grundy numbers** ([7.5](../../phase-7/7-5-game-theory/)): is it one pile (labels win/lose) or a sum of independent games (xor of numbers)?
- **Tree DP or DAG DP** ([7.1](../../phase-7/7-1-tree-dp/), [7.2](../../phase-7/7-2-dag-dp/)): is the graph a tree (merge children) or a general DAG (topological order)?

## 8. Worked examples

The two examples show the routine on problems from the review bank, written out in full, with the code. Notice how little of each solution is "code": the work is in steps 1 to 3.

### Example 1: Bomber

[AtCoder ABC176 E: Bomber](https://atcoder.jp/contests/abc176/tasks/abc176_e). In short: a grid of $H$ rows and $W$ columns has $M \le 3 \cdot 10^5$ targets; one bomb destroys every target in its row and its column; find the most targets one bomb can destroy.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$H$, $W$ and $M$ are all up to $3 \cdot 10^5$, so $H \cdot W$ cells cannot be tried ($9 \cdot 10^{10}$). We want about $M + H + W$ steps.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Try every cell of the grid ([topic 0.5](../../phase-0/0-5-brute-force/)): $H \cdot W$ cells, each needing a count; far too slow.
- Count the targets in every row and every column ([topic 0.6](../../phase-0/0-6-stl-toolbox/), a map or an array of counts) and combine: $M + H + W$ steps.

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The bomb on $(r, c)$ destroys `rows[r]` + `cols[c]` targets, and one target too many is counted if the cell $(r, c)$ itself holds a target. So the best bomb is on a crossing of a busiest row and a busiest column, unless all such crossings hold a target. Decision: the counts, with one check of the crossings.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

Let $R$ be the largest row count and $C$ the largest column count. For any cell the value is at most $R + C$, and it equals $R + C$ exactly when the cell lies in a busiest row and a busiest column and holds no target. If some such crossing holds no target, the answer is $R + C$. If every crossing holds a target, then every cell on a crossing has the value $R + C - 1$, and any other cell has at most $R + C - 1$ too (it misses a busiest row or a busiest column, which loses at least one), so the answer is $R + C - 1$. The number of crossings is the number of busiest rows times the number of busiest columns; we count the targets on crossings and compare.

### Example 2: Subarrays with K Different Integers

[LeetCode 992: Subarrays with K Different Integers](https://leetcode.com/problems/subarrays-with-k-different-integers/). In short: an array of $n \le 2 \cdot 10^4$ integers and a number $k$; count the subarrays with exactly $k$ different values.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$n = 2 \cdot 10^4$ allows $n^2 = 4 \cdot 10^8$ in the original problem (a double loop with a set), but we aim for $O(n \log n)$ or $O(n)$ so that the idea works for $n = 2 \cdot 10^5$.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Two loops and a set ([topic 0.5](../../phase-0/0-5-brute-force/)): $O(n^2)$ with a hash set.
- A sliding window ([topic 1.3](../../phase-1/1-3-two-pointers/)): the question is about contiguous segments and all values are non-negative counts.
- Prefix sums ([topic 1.2](../../phase-1/1-2-prefix-sums/)): there is no sum, so nothing to subtract.

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

Does a shrinking window stay valid? For "exactly $k$ different" it does not: the window $[1, 2, 1]$ with $k = 2$ is valid, and the shorter window $[1]$ is not. But for "at most $k$ different" it does: removing an element never adds a value. So the card applies to *at most*, not to *exactly*. Decision: the sliding window for "at most $k$", and exactly$(k)$ = atMost$(k)$ − atMost$(k - 1)$.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

Every subarray has some number $d$ of different values, so the subarrays with $d \le k$ are those with $d = k$ and those with $d \le k - 1$. Hence exactly$(k)$ = atMost$(k)$ − atMost$(k - 1)$. For atMost, for each right end $r$ the smallest left end $\ell(r)$ such that $[\ell(r), r]$ has at most $k$ different values never decreases when $r$ grows (a longer window does not have fewer values, so the left end only moves right), and every left end between $\ell(r)$ and $r$ also works, so there are $r - \ell(r) + 1$ good subarrays ending at $r$. Moving $\ell$ only to the right gives $O(n \log n)$ with a map (or $O(n)$ with an array of counts).
