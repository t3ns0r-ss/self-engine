## 1. Why this topic exists

Here is a small problem. A company is a tree with $n = 2 \cdot 10^5$ employees; employee 1 is the boss and every other employee has one direct boss. You are given $q = 2 \cdot 10^5$ questions: "who is the lowest boss that both $a$ and $b$ report to (directly or through others)?", and "how many reporting lines are between $a$ and $b$?"

The parents of [topic 4.5](../4-5-trees/) let you climb from a vertex to the root, one step at a time. To answer one question, climb the deeper vertex until both are at the same depth, then climb both together until they meet. A chain of $2 \cdot 10^5$ employees makes each climb $2 \cdot 10^5$ steps, and $q$ questions cost up to $4 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps.

The key idea: do not climb one step at a time. For every vertex store its ancestor $1, 2, 4, 8, \dots$ levels up, about $\log_2 n \approx 18$ of them. Each ancestor is two jumps of the previous size, so the table is built from the one before it. To go up $k$ levels, write $k$ in binary and take the jumps of the sizes that are in $k$: at most 18 jumps. To find the lowest common ancestor, first lift the deeper vertex to the depth of the other; then, from the largest jump to the smallest, jump both vertices whenever the two ancestors reached are *different*: they stop just below the meeting point, whose parent is the answer. This is **binary lifting**, and every question takes about 40 steps instead of $2 \cdot 10^5$. The distance between $a$ and $b$ is then $\text{depth}(a) + \text{depth}(b) - 2\,\text{depth}(\text{lca})$, and the same jump table can carry the minimum or the largest edge on the way up.

## 3. Definitions

- In a rooted tree ([topic 4.5](../4-5-trees/)) the **$k$-th ancestor** of $v$ is the vertex reached by climbing $k$ parents from $v$; it does not exist when $k$ is larger than the depth of $v$ (the code uses 0 for "none"). The 0-th ancestor is $v$ itself.
- The **lowest common ancestor** (LCA) of $u$ and $v$ is the common ancestor with the largest depth. Since a vertex counts as its own ancestor, $\text{lca}(u, v) = u$ when $u$ is an ancestor of $v$.
- **Binary lifting** stores, for every vertex $v$ and every $j = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, the $2^j$-th ancestor $\text{up}[j][v]$ (the **jump table**). It needs $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ levels, which is 18 for $n = 2 \cdot 10^5$, so about $3.6 \cdot 10^6$ numbers.
- The path between $u$ and $v$ goes up from $u$ to $\text{lca}(u, v)$ and then down to $v$. The **weighted depth** $\text{wd}[v]$ is the sum of the weights on the path from the root to $v$ (equal to the depth when all weights are 1).
- The code units number the vertices from 1, use 0 for "no vertex", and take the root of the tree to be the vertex named in the call.


## 4. Theory and proofs

### 4.1 Jumps by powers of two

**Theorem 4.6.1 (binary lifting).** Let $T$ be a tree rooted as in Theorem 4.5.1, with $\text{depth}$ and $\text{parent}$ (0 for the root). Let $L$ be the least number with $2^L > n$. Put $\text{up}[0][v] = \text{parent}[v]$, $\text{up}[j][0] = 0$ and $\text{up}[j][v] = \text{up}[j-1][\text{up}[j-1][v]]$ for $j = 1, \dots, L - 1$. Then:

(a) $\text{up}[j][v]$ is the $2^j$-th ancestor of $v$, or 0 if $v$ has fewer than $2^j$ ancestors;

(b) if $k \le \text{depth}[v]$ and $k = 2^{j_1} + 2^{j_2} + \dots$ is the binary form of $k$, then applying the jumps $\text{up}[j_1], \text{up}[j_2], \dots$ one after the other (in any order) gives the $k$-th ancestor of $v$; if $k > \text{depth}[v]$ there is none;

(c) the table is built in $O(n \log n)$ time and each $k$-th ancestor takes $O(\log n)$ time.

:::tip[In plain words]
To climb $2^j$ levels, climb $2^{j-1}$ levels twice. So write down, for every vertex, where one step, two steps, four steps, eight steps and so on lead, each level built from the one before it. Any number of steps is a sum of different powers of two, so any climb is at most about 18 jumps.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The tree $1\!-\!2\!-\!3\!-\!4\!-\!5\!-\!6$ (a path rooted at 1) with the extra edges $2\!-\!7$ and $7\!-\!8$. For vertex 6: $\text{up}[0][6] = 5$, $\text{up}[1][6] = \text{up}[0][5] = 4$, $\text{up}[2][6] = \text{up}[1][4] = 2$. The 5th ancestor of 6 is reached by $5 = 4 + 1$: $\text{up}[2][6] = 2$, then $\text{up}[0][2] = 1$. The 6th ancestor does not exist (the depth of 6 is 5), so the answer is 0.

**Proof.**
1. *Claim (a) by induction on $j$.* For $j = 0$ it is the definition of the parent. For $j \ge 1$, the $2^j$-th ancestor of $v$ is reached by climbing $2^{j-1}$ levels from $v$ and then $2^{j-1}$ levels from there; this is $\text{up}[j-1][\text{up}[j-1][v]]$ by the hypothesis. If $v$ has fewer than $2^{j-1}$ ancestors, the first jump gives 0, and $\text{up}[j-1][0] = 0$; if the second jump falls short, it gives 0 as well. In both cases there are fewer than $2^j$ ancestors, and the result 0 is right.
2. *Claim (b).* Each jump $\text{up}[j]$ climbs exactly $2^j$ levels when it exists. The jumps of the binary form of $k$ climb $\sum 2^{j_i} = k$ levels in total, and their order does not matter because the total number of levels is the same. When $k \le \text{depth}[v]$, no intermediate jump goes above the root, since the partial sums are at most $k$.
3. The digits of $k$ need at most $L$ levels: since $k \le \text{depth}[v] \le n - 1 < 2^L$, all its binary digits $j_i$ are below $L$.
4. *Claim (c).* The table has $L \le \lceil \log_2 n \rceil + 1$ rows of $n + 1$ entries, each computed with two lookups. A query makes at most $L$ jumps. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The check $k \le \text{depth}[v]$ first. Otherwise a $k$ with a binary digit beyond the table would silently use the wrong jumps.
2. The entry for "none" is 0 and $\text{up}[j][0] = 0$, so that jumps past the root stay at 0.
3. Memory: $18 \cdot 2 \cdot 10^5 = 3.6 \cdot 10^6$ numbers, which is fine for `int`.
:::

The first example prints the ancestors of vertex 6 at $k = 0, \dots, 6$ (ending with 0) and two more queries on vertex 8. The second is a single vertex (the 0-th ancestor is itself, the first does not exist). ### 4.2 The lowest common ancestor

**Theorem 4.6.2 (lowest common ancestor).** With the table of Theorem 4.6.1, let $u$ and $v$ be vertices with $\text{depth}[u] \ge \text{depth}[v]$. Let $u'$ be the $(\text{depth}[u] - \text{depth}[v])$-th ancestor of $u$. If $u' = v$, then $\text{lca}(u, v) = v$. Otherwise, for $j = L - 1$ down to 0: if $\text{up}[j][u'] \ne \text{up}[j][v']$, replace $u'$ by $\text{up}[j][u']$ and $v'$ by $\text{up}[j][v']$ (start with $v' = v$). Then $\text{lca}(u, v) = \text{parent}[u']$. The time is $O(\log n)$.

:::tip[In plain words]
First bring the deeper vertex up to the level of the other. If they are the same vertex, that is the answer. Otherwise they are on the same level but different, so climb both together; look from the biggest jump to the smallest, and take a jump only if you would still land on two different vertices. You stop just below the meeting point, and its parent is the answer.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The tree of the previous theorem (path $1-\dots-6$ and the branch $2\!-\!7\!-\!8$), $u = 6$ (depth 5) and $v = 8$ (depth 3). Lift 6 by 2 levels to $u' = 4$. The 8th and 4th ancestors of 4 and 8 are both 0 (equal), no jump; the 2nd ancestors $\text{up}[1][4] = 2$ and $\text{up}[1][8] = 2$ are equal, no jump; the 1st ancestors 3 and 7 differ, jump: $u' = 3$, $v' = 7$. The parent of 3 is 2: $\text{lca}(6, 8) = 2$.

**Proof.**
1. After the lift, $u'$ and $v$ are on the same level. The lca of $u$ and $v$ has depth at most $\text{depth}[v]$, so it is an ancestor of $u'$ (the ancestors of $u$ that are not deeper than $v$ are exactly the ancestors of $u'$), and $\text{lca}(u, v) = \text{lca}(u', v)$. If $u' = v$, then $v$ is an ancestor of $u$ and is the lca.
2. Otherwise $u' \ne v$ are at the same depth. Let $l$ be their lca and $h = \text{depth}[u'] - \text{depth}[l] \ge 1$. For $k \ge 0$ let $P(k)$ say: "the $k$-th ancestors of $u'$ and $v$ differ". Below the lca the ancestors are different vertices, and from the lca upwards they are the same vertex, so $P(k)$ holds exactly for $k < h$. In particular, if $P(k)$ holds then $P(k')$ holds for every smaller $k'$.
3. Let $c$ be the number of levels the loop has climbed so far. We show by induction on the loop that after the step $j$, $c \le h - 1$ and $h - 1 - c < 2^j$. Before the loop, $c = 0$ and $h - 1 < n \le 2^L$ hold, so the claim holds for $j = L$.
4. In the step $j$: before it, $h - 1 - c < 2^{j+1}$. The jump is taken exactly when the $2^j$-th ancestors of the current vertices differ, i.e., when $P(c + 2^j)$ holds, which means $c + 2^j \le h - 1$. In this case $c$ grows by $2^j$ and $h - 1 - c < 2^j$ holds afterwards. Otherwise $c + 2^j > h - 1$ and $h - 1 - c < 2^j$ already.
5. After the step $j = 0$ we have $h - 1 - c < 1$, so $c = h - 1$: the current vertices are the children of $l$ on the way to $u'$ and $v$, and the parent of $u'$ is $l$. Each step is $O(1)$, so the time is $O(L)$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The lift to equal depths comes first; the loop compares the ancestors at the same level.
2. The loop goes from the largest jump to the smallest. From the smallest to the largest the monotone test $P$ cannot be used greedily.
3. Jumps past the root give 0 on both sides, equal, so they are never taken.
:::

The second is a single vertex. The check marks the ancestors of $u$ by climbing and then climbs from $v$ to the first marked vertex, for every pair of vertices of twenty thousand random trees.

### 4.3 Distances in a tree

**Theorem 4.6.3 (distance).** Let $T$ be a tree with non-negative weights and a root, and let $\text{wd}[v]$ be the weighted depth of $v$. Then for all vertices $u$ and $v$,
$$d(u, v) = \text{wd}[u] + \text{wd}[v] - 2\,\text{wd}[\text{lca}(u, v)].$$
With all weights 1 this is $\text{depth}[u] + \text{depth}[v] - 2\,\text{depth}[\text{lca}(u, v)]$, and one query takes $O(\log n)$ time after $O(n \log n)$ preparation.

:::tip[In plain words]
The trip from $u$ to $v$ goes up to their meeting point and down again. The two ends' distances from the root count the stretch above the meeting point twice, so take it off twice.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The tree with the edges $1\!-\!2$ (3), $2\!-\!3$ (4), $2\!-\!4$ (1), $4\!-\!5$ (6), $1\!-\!6$ (2), rooted at 1. The weighted depths of $3$ and $5$ are $7$ and $10$; their lca is vertex 2 with weighted depth 3. So $d(3, 5) = 7 + 10 - 6 = 11$, which is the path $4 + 1 + 6$.

**Proof.**
1. Let $l = \text{lca}(u, v)$. The vertices on the path from $u$ up to $l$ are ancestors of $u$, and the vertices on the path from $v$ up to $l$ are ancestors of $v$.
2. Below $l$ the two climbs use different children of $l$ (a shared vertex below $l$ would be a common ancestor deeper than $l$), so they have no vertex in common except $l$. Together they form a path from $u$ to $v$, which is the only path (Theorem 4.5.1(a)).
3. The weight from $u$ up to $l$ is $\text{wd}[u] - \text{wd}[l]$ (the root-to-$u$ path passes through $l$), and likewise $\text{wd}[v] - \text{wd}[l]$ on the other side. The sum is the formula. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The weights are the same in both directions along an edge; for a directed cost on each edge the formula does not apply.
2. $\text{wd}$ is computed in the visit order (parents first), with 64-bit numbers.
:::

### 4.4 The smallest edge on a path

**Theorem 4.6.4 (aggregates on a path).** Let every non-root vertex $v$ have a weight $w_v$ on the edge to its parent. Put $\text{mn}[0][v] = w_v$ and $\text{mn}[j][v] = \min(\text{mn}[j-1][v], \text{mn}[j-1][\text{up}[j-1][v]])$ (just the first term when $\text{up}[j-1][v] = 0$). Then $\text{mn}[j][v]$ is the smallest weight among the $2^j$ edges climbed by $\text{up}[j][v]$ (when $\text{up}[j][v] \ne 0$). The smallest weight on the path from $u$ to $v$ is the minimum of the values $\text{mn}[j][\cdot]$ over the jumps made by the search of Theorem 4.6.2, together with the two edges $\text{mn}[0][u']$ and $\text{mn}[0][v']$ at the end. The same works for the largest weight, the sum or the XOR in place of the minimum. The time is $O(\log n)$ per query after $O(n \log n)$ preparation.

:::tip[In plain words]
Along with each jump of $2^j$ levels, remember the smallest edge it passes over. A path is cut into exactly the same jumps as in the search for the meeting point, so the smallest edge of the path is the smallest of the smallest edges of the jumps. The idea works for anything that can be combined piece by piece: a sum, a maximum, a minimum.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The tree of Theorem 4.6.3. For the path from 3 to 5: vertex 5 has depth 3 and vertex 3 has depth 2, so 5 is lifted by one level to vertex 4, collecting the edge $5\!-\!4$ of weight 6. The vertices 4 and 3 are different, but their parents are the same vertex 2, so no jump of the loop is taken; the last step adds the two edges $4\!-\!2$ (1) and $3\!-\!2$ (4). The minimum of $6, 1, 4$ is 1.

**Proof.**
1. The claim about $\text{mn}[j][v]$ follows by induction on $j$ as in Theorem 4.6.1(a): the $2^j$ edges climbed by $\text{up}[j][v]$ are the $2^{j-1}$ edges climbed from $v$ and the $2^{j-1}$ edges climbed from $\text{up}[j-1][v]$.
2. The path from $u$ to $v$ consists of the edges climbed from $u$ in the lift to the depth of $v$, the edges climbed in the jumps of the loop on each side, and the two last edges to $l$ (the parent steps from the children of $l$).
3. Each of these edges is climbed by exactly one jump, so the pieces cover the path without overlap, and the minimum of the pieces is the minimum of the path. For the sum or the XOR the pieces are added, with no overlap either. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Every edge of the path is covered exactly once for sums and XOR; for minimum and maximum an overlap would be harmless, but the pieces here do not overlap.
2. A weight "INF" is used for the root's missing edge and is never taken into a piece.
3. Memory: one extra table of the size of the jump table.
:::

The check compares the answers with a search from each vertex that records the smallest weight on the way, for every pair of vertices of twenty thousand random weighted trees.

### 4.5 Adding along many paths

**Theorem 4.6.5 (counting along paths offline).** Let a rooted tree and paths $\{u_i, v_i\}$ be given. Set $c[x] = (\text{number of path ends at } x) - 2 \cdot (\text{number of paths whose lca is } x)$. Then for every non-root vertex $v$, the number of paths that use the edge between $v$ and its parent is the sum of $c$ over the subtree of $v$. With the subtree sums of Theorem 4.5.2 the whole answer takes $O((n + q) \log n)$ time for $q$ paths.

:::tip[In plain words]
A path uses the edge above $v$ exactly when one of its ends is inside the subtree of $v$ and the other is outside. Put $+1$ at both ends and $-2$ at the meeting point: the contributions of a path cancel inside every subtree that holds the whole path, and leave exactly $+1$ in each subtree that holds just one end.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The tree with the edges $1\!-\!2$, $1\!-\!3$, $2\!-\!4$, $2\!-\!5$, $3\!-\!6$ and the paths $\{4, 5\}$, $\{4, 6\}$, $\{5, 6\}$. The lcas are 2, 1 and 1. The ends give $c[4] = 2$, $c[5] = 2$, $c[6] = 2$ (each is an end of two paths), and the lcas give $c[2] = -2$ and $c[1] = -4$. The sums over the subtrees of $4, 5, 6$ are $2$ each, over the subtree of $3$ it is $2$ (vertex 6), and over the subtree of $2$ it is $2 + 2 - 2 = 2$. Each of the five edges is used by two paths; for example the edge $2\!-\!1$ is used by $\{4, 6\}$ and $\{5, 6\}$.

**Proof.**
1. Fix a vertex $v \ne$ root, and a path with ends $a$, $b$ and lca $l$. The edge between $v$ and its parent is on the path exactly when exactly one of $a$ and $b$ lies in the subtree $S(v)$ (the path has to enter or leave $S(v)$ through this edge, once).
2. Both ends in $S(v)$: then $l$, an ancestor of both that is on the path between them, lies in $S(v)$ too (the path between two vertices of a subtree stays inside it). The path contributes $2 - 2 = 0$ to the sum over $S(v)$.
3. Exactly one end in $S(v)$: then $l$ is not in $S(v)$ (otherwise the other end, a descendant of $l$, would be in $S(v)$ too). The contribution is $1$, and the edge is used.
4. No end in $S(v)$: then $l$ is not in $S(v)$ either (an lca in $S(v)$ would have its descendants, the ends, in $S(v)$). The contribution is $0$, and the edge is not used.
5. Summing over all paths gives the claim. The subtree sums come from Theorem 4.5.2, and the lcas from Theorem 4.6.2. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. All paths are known before the answer is needed (offline), and the values are added only, never asked in between. For questions interleaved with changes, a structure for updates is needed (a later phase).
2. The counts for the vertices are put on the vertices; the number for an edge is stored at the lower vertex.
3. The sums are 64-bit when there are many paths.
:::

## 6. Templates

Seven templates. Each shows only the function that matters; the program around it reads the input. The functions that take a `Lifting` use the structure of Theorem 4.6.1 and the function `lca` of Theorem 4.6.2 (built over the walk of Theorem 4.5.1), and say so. Vertices are numbered from 1. Each template is checked against a brute force with a different method (using the pointers one at a time, marking the ancestors, a walk for every question) on thousands of random small inputs, and on fixed tests with a single vertex, a chain, repeated questions and sums near the limit of 64 bits.

### Where do the teleporters lead after $k$ steps? (Theorem 4.6.1)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.6/kth-successor" />

**How it works.** `up[j][v]` is the planet reached from `v` after $2^j$ teleporters; row 0 is the teleporters themselves, and each further row is two jumps of the row before (`up[j-1][up[j-1][v]]`). For a query, the set bits of `k` say which jumps to take, and the order of the jumps does not matter. With $k \le 10^9$ there are 31 rows, because $2^{31} > 10^9$. A teleporter that leads to the planet itself is a pointer to itself, and the jumps stay there.

**Alternative formulation.** To find the first planet on the way with some property that never goes from true to false (for example "has index at most $x$" along a path to the root), jump from the largest $j$ to the smallest and take a jump only while the planet reached still does not have the property.

### Lowest common boss from a parent array (Theorem 4.6.2)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.6/lca-queries" />

**How it works.** When every boss has a smaller number than the employee, the depths and the first row of the table come straight from the array in one loop with no walk. For a query the deeper employee is lifted by the difference of the depths, bit by bit; if the two are now the same employee, that is the answer, and otherwise both jump from the largest level to the smallest whenever the ancestors differ, and the answer is the parent of the place where they stop.

**Alternative formulation.** If the parents are not smaller than their children, build `adj` and use `rootAt` and `Lifting` as in the theorem. For many queries and a very large $n$ the table of $n \log n$ numbers can be replaced by the Euler tour and a table of minima over a range (not covered here).

### Distances between towns (Theorem 4.6.3)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.6/tree-distances" />

**How it works.** `L.depth` is the number of roads from the root, and `lca` finds the meeting point of the two towns by the lifting of Theorem 4.6.2. The trip goes up from `a` to the meeting point and down to `b`, so the number of roads is the sum of the two depths minus twice the depth of the meeting point.

**Alternative formulation.** For roads with lengths, replace `depth` by the weighted depth `wd` computed in the visit order (parents first) and use 64-bit numbers. The distances between all pairs of a few thousand towns can also be read off a table of Floyd–Warshall, but only for small $n$.

### Is the town on the way? (Theorem 4.6.3)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.6/on-the-way" />

**How it works.** A town `c` is on the trip between `a` and `b` exactly when going from `a` to `c` and then from `c` to `b` is as long as going from `a` to `b` directly: $d(a, c) + d(c, b) = d(a, b)$. Each distance is `wd[u] + wd[v] - 2 * wd[lca]`. This needs every road to have a positive length: with a road of length 0 a town off the path can give the same sum.

**Alternative formulation.** With unit lengths the same test uses depths. A direct test without distances is to ask whether `c` is an ancestor of `a` or of `b` and a descendant of the lowest common ancestor, which needs only the ancestor test of Theorem 4.5.3.

### The weakest road on a trip (Theorem 4.6.4)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.6/path-minimum" />

**How it works.** `mn[0][v]` is the limit of the road from `v` to its parent, and `mn[j][v]` is the smallest limit among the $2^j$ roads climbed from `v` (the first half from `v`, the second half from the place `up[j-1][v]`). A query lifts the deeper town, taking the minimum of each jump it makes, then jumps both while the ancestors differ, again taking minima, and adds the last road on each side. The minimum of the pieces is the minimum of the trip, because the pieces cover the roads once each.

**Alternative formulation.** For the largest limit replace `min` by `max`; for the total length of the roads replace it by a sum (then `LLONG_MAX` is not used as a sentinel for missing roads). For a maximum over a whole trip with many equal queries, sort the roads and use the Kruskal order of topic 4.4.

### Candies by jumps (Theorem 4.6.4)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.6/candies-jump" />

**How it works.** The vertex is the position `X mod N`; after a step the position is `(v + A[v]) % N` and `A[v]` candies have been added. `next[j][v]` is the position after $2^j$ steps and `gain[j][v]` is the number of candies added during them; the second half of a jump starts at the position `mid` reached after the first half. For $K \le 10^{12}$ there are 41 rows. The set bits of `K` give the jumps, and the sums of the gains give the answer, which fits in 64 bits ($10^{12} \cdot 10^6 = 10^{18}$).

**Alternative formulation.** The positions repeat after at most $N$ steps, so the walk enters a cycle; finding the cycle and using its total gives the same answer in $O(N)$ time, and is the simpler solution when only one $K$ is asked.

### How often is each road used? (Theorem 4.6.5)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.6/road-usage" />

**How it works.** Every trip adds 1 at its two ends and subtracts 2 at its lowest common ancestor. The backward pass over the visit order then turns each entry into the sum over the subtree of the vertex, and that sum is the number of trips that use the road between the vertex and its parent. The count for a road in the input order is read at its lower end, the end whose parent is the other end.

**Alternative formulation.** To count vertices instead of roads, put $+1$ at both ends, $-1$ at the lowest common ancestor and $-1$ at its parent, and read the sums at the vertices. For counts with different values per trip, add the value instead of 1.

## 7. Bug catalogue

Each bug below was made by changing a template from Section 6 and caught by `scripts/stress.sh`. The inputs shown are small failing inputs, checked against the brute force.

### Bug: too few levels in the table

Template: where do the teleporters lead after $k$ steps?

```cpp
int n = next.size() - 1, levels = 3;                    // instead of levels = 31
```

Input: `2 1`, then the teleporters `1 1`, then the question `2 8`.

Wrong output `2`, correct output `1`. Planet 2 leads to planet 1 and planet 1 leads to itself, so after any $k \ge 1$ steps the answer is 1. With 3 levels only the bits 0, 1 and 2 of $k$ are used; $8$ is bit 3, so it is ignored and the traveller stays on planet 2 (Theorem 4.6.1: the table needs a level for every bit of the largest $k$).

**Fix:** take levels with $2^{\text{levels}} > k_{\max}$; here 31 for $k \le 10^9$.

### Bug: a jump of $2^j$ made from one half

Template: where do the teleporters lead after $k$ steps?

```cpp
up[j][v] = up[j - 1][v];                                // instead of up[j - 1][up[j - 1][v]]
```

Input: `3 1`, then the teleporters `2 3 1`, then the question `3 9`.

Wrong output `2`, correct output `3`. The planets form a cycle $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$. Nine steps are three full turns, so the traveller is back on planet 3. With the wrong rule every level is the same single step, and the bits of 9 (levels 0 and 3) add up to two steps: planet 3 → 1 → 2 (Theorem 4.6.1, step 2: $2^j$ steps are two jumps of $2^{j-1}$).

**Fix:** jump twice with the previous level.

### Bug: lifting the shallower vertex

Template: lowest common boss from a parent array.

```cpp
                                                        // the swap "if (depth[a] < depth[b]) swap(a, b);" is missing
```

Input: `3 1`, then the bosses `1 2` (employee 2 under 1, employee 3 under 2), then the question `1 3`.

Wrong output `0`, correct output `1`. Here $a = 1$ is the top and $b = 3$ is two levels lower, so $\text{depth}[a] - \text{depth}[b] = -2$. The loop reads the bits of a negative number and lifts $a$ above the root, onto the dummy vertex 0 (Theorem 4.6.2, step 1: lift the *deeper* vertex).

**Fix:** swap so that `a` is the deeper one before the difference is taken.

### Bug: returning the vertex instead of its parent

Template: lowest common boss from a parent array.

```cpp
answer.push_back(a);                                    // instead of a == b ? a : up[0][a]
```

Input: `3 1`, then the bosses `1 1`, then the question `3 2`.

Wrong output `3`, correct output `1`. Employees 2 and 3 are both directly under 1 and are different. The jumping loop stops one step *below* the common ancestor (their parents are equal, so no jump is made), and `a` is still employee 3 (Theorem 4.6.2, step 3: the answer is the parent of the stopping place).

**Fix:** return `a` only if the two were equal after the lift, otherwise `up[0][a]`.

### Bug: jumping while the ancestor exists, not while the ancestors differ

Template: lowest common boss from a parent array.

```cpp
if (up[j][a] != 0) a = up[j][a], b = up[j][b];          // instead of up[j][a] != up[j][b]
```

Input: `4 1`, then the bosses `1 2 2`, then the question `3 4`.

Wrong output `1`, correct output `2`. Employees 3 and 4 are both under employee 2, which is under 1. The test "does the ancestor exist" lets both vertices jump to employee 1, which is a common ancestor but not the lowest one. The test must ask whether the two ancestors are *different*, so that the jump never goes past the meeting place.

**Fix:** jump only when `up[j][a] != up[j][b]`.

### Bug: forgetting the 2 in the distance

Template: distances between towns.

```cpp
answer.push_back(L.depth[a] + L.depth[b] - L.depth[lca(L, a, b)]);   // instead of 2 * L.depth[...]
```

Input: `2 1`, then the road `2 1`, then the question `2 2`.

Wrong output `1`, correct output `0`. A town is at distance 0 from itself. With $a = b = 2$ the formula gives $1 + 1 - 1 = 1$: the road above the common ancestor is counted from *both* ends, so it must be taken away twice (Theorem 4.6.3).

**Fix:** subtract $2 \cdot \text{depth}[\text{lca}]$.

### Bug: the roads below the meeting place are lost

Template: the weakest road on a trip.

```cpp
return best;                                            // instead of min({best, mn[0][a], mn[0][b]})
```

Input: `4 1`, then the roads `3 1 6`, `4 3 5`, `2 3 9` (limits 6, 5, 9), then the question `4 2`.

Wrong output `9223372036854775807` (the starting "infinity"), correct output `5`. Towns 4 and 2 are both children of 3, so the loop makes no jump at all, and the two roads `4-3` and `2-3` are the only ones on the trip. They are still waiting under the two vertices (Theorem 4.6.4, step 3: the last road from each side is not covered by any jump).

**Fix:** finish with the roads from `a` and `b` to their parent.

### Bug: the road above the common ancestor is counted

Template: the weakest road on a trip.

```cpp
                                                        // the line "if (a == b) return best;" is missing
```

Input: `3 2`, then the roads `3 1 3` and `2 3 7`, then the questions `2 3` and `2 3`.

Wrong output `3`, correct output `7`. Town 3 is above town 2, so the trip is the single road `2-3` with limit 7. After lifting, `a` and `b` are both town 3; without the early return the final line looks at the road from 3 to its parent as well (limit 3), and that road is not on the trip.

**Fix:** when the two are equal after the lift, the answer is what the lift collected.

### Bug: the second half of a jump is the same as the first

Template: candies by jumps.

```cpp
gain[j][v] = gain[j - 1][v] * 2;                        // instead of gain[j - 1][v] + gain[j - 1][mid]
```

Input: `2 4`, then `1 3`.

Wrong output `4`, correct output `8`. The dish gets $1$ (from the dish at 0), then $3$ (at 1), then $1$, then $3$: in total $8$. The two halves of a jump start at different vertices, so the second half must be read at `mid`, the place where the first half ended (Theorem 4.6.4, step 2).

**Fix:** add the gain stored at `mid`.

### Bug: −1 at the lowest common ancestor

Template: how often is each road used?

```cpp
count[a]++, count[b]++, count[lca(L, a, b)] -= 1;       // instead of -= 2
```

Input: `2 1`, then the road `2 1`, then the trip `2 2`.

Wrong output `1`, correct output `0`. A trip from a town to itself uses no road. The two $+1$ marks at town 2 are cancelled only by $-2$ at the lowest common ancestor, which is town 2 itself; with $-1$ one mark is left, and the subtree sum of town 2 reports a trip through the road above it (Theorem 4.6.5, step 1).

**Fix:** subtract 2: one for each of the two paths that stop at the lowest common ancestor.

### Bug: adding up in the wrong order

Template: how often is each road used?

```cpp
for (int i = 1; i < n; i++) count[t.parent[t.order[i]]] += count[t.order[i]];   // instead of from n - 1 down to 1
```

Input: `4 1`, then the roads `1 2`, `3 2`, `3 4`, then the trip `4 3`.

Wrong output `-1 0 1`, correct output `0 0 1`. Only the road `3-4` is on the trip. The root is 1, the order is $1, 2, 3, 4$, and the marks are $+1$ at 4 and $-1$ at 3. Going forwards, vertex 2 collects from vertex 3 *before* vertex 3 has collected from vertex 4, so the road `1-2` gets the $-1$ and never the matching $+1$. The children must be finished before their parent (Theorem 4.5.2, step 2).

**Fix:** go from the last vertex of the order to the first.
