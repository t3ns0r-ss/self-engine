## 1. Why this topic exists

Here is a small problem. There are $n = 400$ piles of stones in a row. Two **neighbouring** piles can be merged into one, which costs their total size; the new pile takes their place. Merge everything into one pile as cheaply as possible.

A natural greedy, "merge the neighbouring pair with the smallest total", fails: for piles $(2, 2, 1, 2)$ it merges $2 + 1$ first and pays $3 + 5 + 7 = 15$, while merging $2 + 2$ and $1 + 2$ first pays $4 + 3 + 7 = 14$. Trying every order of merges ([topic 3.1](../3-1-backtracking/)) is hopeless: there are $(n-1)!$ orders.

The key idea: look at the **last** merge. It joins a pile made from piles $1..k$ with a pile made from piles $k+1..n$, for some split point $k$, and the two sides never interacted before. So the best cost for the whole row comes from the best costs of two shorter segments. The states are the segments $[l, r]$, about $n^2 / 2 = 8 \cdot 10^4$ of them, each trying up to $n$ split points: $O(n^3) \approx 2 \cdot 10^7$ steps. This is **interval DP**: tables over segments, filled from short segments to long ones.

## 3. Definitions

- A **segment** (or interval) $[l, r]$ of a sequence $a_1, \dots, a_n$ is the contiguous part $a_l, \dots, a_r$; its **length** is $r - l + 1$. There are $n(n+1)/2$ non-empty segments.
- **Interval DP** is DP whose states are segments, where the value of a segment depends only on shorter segments inside it.
- A **split point** of $[l, r]$ is a $k$ with $l \le k < r$, dividing the segment into $[l, k]$ and $[k+1, r]$.
- A **palindrome** reads the same forwards and backwards. A **palindromic subsequence** is a subsequence that is a palindrome. Example: "abcab" has the palindromic subsequences "aba", "bcb", "aca", "bab" of length 3, and none longer.
- In a two-player game where both play as well as possible, the **score difference** of a position is (the total of the player to move) − (the total of the other player) from that position to the end.

## 4. Theory and proofs

### 4.1 Segments as states

**Theorem 3.6.1 (filling by length).** Suppose the value $f[l][r]$ of every segment is determined by the values of segments strictly inside it (shorter segments $[l', r'] \subset [l, r]$), plus base values for segments of length 1 (or 0). Then computing the segments in order of increasing length computes every value correctly. With $n$ elements there are $O(n^2)$ states; if each takes $O(n)$ work, the total is $O(n^3)$.

*Proof.* Number the segments by length, breaking ties arbitrarily. Every transition goes from a strictly shorter segment to a longer one, so this numbering is an acyclic order, and Theorem 3.2.3 applies. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. A segment's value uses only shorter segments. Filling row by row in $l$ from 1 to $n$ is **wrong** here: $f[l][r]$ needs $f[l+1][r]$, which comes later in that order. Loop over the length outside (or over $l$ from $n$ down to 1, with $r$ from $l$ up).
2. The budget: $n \le 500$ for $O(n^3)$, $n \le 5000$ for $O(n^2)$.
:::

### 4.2 The best split point

**Theorem 3.6.2 (split point).** Let $\text{best}[l][r]$ be the least cost of merging the piles $l..r$ into one, where merging two neighbouring piles costs their total. Then $\text{best}[l][l] = 0$ and
$$\text{best}[l][r] = \min_{l \le k < r} \left(\text{best}[l][k] + \text{best}[k+1][r]\right) + S(l, r),$$
where $S(l, r) = a_l + \dots + a_r$ is computed with prefix sums. Time $O(n^3)$.

*Proof.* Consider any way to merge the piles $l..r$. Its last merge joins two piles; since only neighbours are merged, each of them was built from a contiguous block, so they are $[l, k]$ and $[k+1, r]$ for some $k$, and the merges inside one block never touched the other. The last merge costs $S(l, r)$ whatever $k$ is. So the total is (cost of merging $[l, k]$) + (cost of merging $[k+1, r]$) + $S(l, r)$, and for a fixed $k$ the two parts are minimised independently (Theorem 3.2.1). Every $k$ is possible, so the minimum over $k$ is the answer. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Some last step splits the segment into **independent** sub-segments: what happens inside one does not change the cost of the other.
2. The cost of the last step depends only on $l$, $r$ (and $k$), not on how the parts were built.
3. Other problems choose a different "last" event: the last balloon burst inside $(l, r)$, the first cut of a stick, the root of a tree on keys $l..r$. The proof is the same once that event splits the segment into independent parts.
:::

Trace for piles $(4, 1, 2, 3)$, entry $(l, r)$ = best cost for piles $l..r$:

| | $r = 1$ | $r = 2$ | $r = 3$ | $r = 4$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $l = 1$ | 0 | 5 | 10 | 19 |
| $l = 2$ | | 0 | 3 | 9 |
| $l = 3$ | | | 0 | 5 |
| $l = 4$ | | | | 0 |

For $[1, 4]$: $k = 1$ gives $0 + 9$, $k = 2$ gives $5 + 5$, $k = 3$ gives $10 + 0$; the minimum is 9, plus $S = 10$: 19.

### 4.3 Taking from the ends

In some games and processes, items are removed only from the two ends of a row. Then whatever remains is always one segment $[l, r]$.

**Theorem 3.6.3 (score difference).** Two players alternately take the first or the last number of a row; each wants to maximise their own total. Let $D[l][r]$ be the score difference for the player to move when the row is $a_l..a_r$. Then $D[l][l] = a_l$ and
$$D[l][r] = \max\left(a_l - D[l+1][r], \ a_r - D[l][r-1]\right).$$
The first player's total is $(S + D[1][n]) / 2$, where $S$ is the sum of all numbers.

*Proof.* By induction on the length. If the player to move takes $a_l$, the opponent faces $[l+1, r]$ and, playing best, ends $D[l+1][r]$ ahead from there; so the mover ends $a_l - D[l+1][r]$ ahead. The same holds for $a_r$. The mover picks the better option. Since the two totals add up to $S$ and differ by $D[1][n]$, the first player's total is $(S + D[1][n])/2$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Every move removes items from the ends only, so the position is one segment (and whose turn it is follows from the length).
2. The game is zero-sum in the sense that maximising your total is the same as maximising the difference (the totals add up to a fixed $S$).
:::

Example: row $(3, 9, 1, 2)$. Greedy "take the larger end" takes 3, the opponent takes 9, then 2 and 1: totals 5 and 10. The table gives $D[1][4] = 7$: take 2 first; the opponent must open the 9 to you (taking 3 or 1), and you finish with 11 against 4.

### 4.4 Palindromes inside segments

**Theorem 3.6.4 (longest palindromic subsequence).** Let $P[l][r]$ be the length of the longest palindromic subsequence of $s_l..s_r$. Then $P[l][l] = 1$, $P[l][l-1] = 0$ (empty segment), and for $l < r$
$$P[l][r] = \begin{cases} P[l+1][r-1] + 2 & \text{if } s_l = s_r,\\ \max(P[l+1][r], \ P[l][r-1]) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

*Proof.* If $s_l \ne s_r$, a palindromic subsequence cannot use both $s_l$ and $s_r$ as its two ends (they differ), and if it used both, they would be its first and last letters. So it avoids $s_l$ or avoids $s_r$, and lies in $[l+1, r]$ or $[l, r-1]$. If $s_l = s_r$, take a longest palindromic subsequence $Z$ of $[l, r]$. If it does not use both ends, replace its first letter by $s_l$ and its last by $s_r$ (or add them if it misses one), as in the exchange argument of Theorem 3.4.4: the result is a palindrome at least as long, made of $s_l$, a palindromic subsequence of $[l+1, r-1]$, and $s_r$. So $P[l][r] = P[l+1][r-1] + 2$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The answer for a segment depends on its two end characters and the inner segments.
2. The same table answers "fewest insertions (or deletions) to make $s$ a palindrome": $n - P[1][n]$. It also equals the LCS of $s$ and its reverse (topic 3.4).
:::

Trace for "abcab": the table gives $P[1][5] = 3$ ("aba", "bcb", "aca" or "bab"). The ends a and b differ, so $P[1][5] = \max(P[2][5], P[1][4]) = \max(3, 3)$; and $P[1][4]$ ("abca") has equal ends a, so it is $P[2][3] + 2 = 1 + 2$.

Another table over segments answers "is $s_l..s_r$ a palindrome?": $\text{pal}[l][r]$ holds when $s_l = s_r$ and $\text{pal}[l+1][r-1]$ (true for lengths 0 and 1). It costs $O(n^2)$ and is the first step of every problem that cuts a string into palindromes.

### 4.5 The first element and its partner

**Theorem 3.6.5 (pairing the first element).** One operation deletes a contiguous block of equal letters (after earlier deletions, letters that were apart may have become neighbours). Let $C[l][r]$ be the fewest operations that delete all of $s_l..s_r$, with $C$ of an empty segment 0. Then
$$C[l][r] = \min\left(1 + C[l+1][r], \ \min_{l < k \le r,\ s_k = s_l} \left(C[l+1][k-1] + C[k][r]\right)\right).$$

*Proof.* Take an optimal sequence of operations for $[l, r]$ and look at the operation that deletes $s_l$.

Case 1: it deletes no other letter. Since $s_l$ is the leftmost letter, no other operation can use it to join letters, so the other operations delete $[l+1, r]$ on their own: at least $C[l+1][r]$ of them, plus one. Conversely, solving $[l+1, r]$ and then deleting $s_l$ alone works.

Case 2: it also deletes other letters; let $s_k$ be the nearest one to the right, so $s_k = s_l$. When this operation happens, $s_l$ and $s_k$ are neighbours, so every letter of $[l+1, k-1]$ was deleted earlier, by operations that stay inside $[l+1, k-1]$ (to reach outside they would have to cross $s_l$ or $s_k$, which are still present): at least $C[l+1][k-1]$ operations. The remaining operations delete $[k, r]$, with $s_l$ riding along in the operation of $s_k$: at least $C[k][r]$ of them. Conversely, delete $[l+1, k-1]$ first; then $s_l$ is the left neighbour of $s_k$, which stays the leftmost letter of $[k, r]$, so adding $s_l$ to the operation that deletes $s_k$ keeps that block contiguous and equal, at no extra cost.

The optimal solution falls into one of the cases, and every case is achievable, so $C[l][r]$ is the minimum over them. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Fixing the partner $k$ of the first element splits the segment into an inside part $(l, k)$ and an outside part $[k, r]$ that do not interact.
2. Joining $s_l$ to the operation of its partner costs nothing extra (here: one block of equal letters stays one block). Problems where the cost depends on the size of the block need an extra index (Remove Boxes keeps the number of equal letters attached on the left).
3. The time is $O(n^3)$: $O(n^2)$ segments, $O(n)$ partners each.
:::

Example: "abaca" takes 3 operations: delete b, delete c, then the three a's have become neighbours and go in one operation. In the recurrence, the first a pairs with the a at position 3: $C[2][2] + C[3][5] = 1 + 2$.

### Cheapest bracketing (Theorem 3.6.2)

<Code code={matrixChain} lang="cpp" title="matrix-chain/solution.cpp" />

**How it works.** The last multiplication of the product $M_l \cdots M_r$ joins the product of $M_l..M_k$ (a $d_{l-1} \times d_k$ matrix) with the product of $M_{k+1}..M_r$ (a $d_k \times d_r$ matrix), for some split point $k$, and costs $d_{l-1} d_k d_r$ whatever happened inside the two parts. So `best[l][r]` is the minimum over $k$ of the two parts plus that cost (Theorem 3.6.2). The length loop is outside, so both parts are always ready (Theorem 3.6.1). Time $O(n^3)$: about $n^3 / 6 = 4.5 \cdot 10^6$ steps for $n = 300$.

**C++ details explained.** The costs are `long long`: one product costs up to $10^6$ and there are up to 299 of them. `best[l][r] = LLONG_MAX` starts the minimum; it is replaced in the first iteration of `k`. The table has $n + 2$ rows and columns so that `best[k + 1][r]` with `k + 1 = r` stays inside.

**Tested.** Stress-tested against plain recursion over every last multiplication, on 5000 random inputs ($n \le 9$, sizes up to 5 or up to 100). There are also 5 fixed tests: one matrix (0), the sizes $10, 30, 5, 60$ (4500, for $(M_1 M_2) M_3$; the other order costs 27000), 300 random sizes (from a separate program), 300 matrices of size $1 \times 1$ (299), and 300 matrices of size $100 \times 100$ ($299 \cdot 10^6$).

**Alternative formulation.** Memoised recursion (Theorem 3.2.4): `solve(l, r)` tries every `k` and caches the result. It visits the same $O(n^2)$ states in the order the recursion needs them, which avoids thinking about the loop order; the recursion depth is at most $n$.

### Taking from the ends (Theorem 3.6.3)

<Code code={endGame} lang="cpp" title="end-game/solution.cpp" />

**How it works.** `diff[l]` holds the score difference of the player to move on the segment of the current length that starts at $l$. For a new length, the segment $[l, r]$ needs $[l + 1, r]$ and $[l, r - 1]$, both one shorter: before `diff[l]` is overwritten it still holds $[l, r - 1]$, and `diff[l + 1]` still holds $[l + 1, r]$ (it is overwritten later in the loop). Taking $a_l$ leaves the opponent $D[l+1][r]$ ahead, so the mover gets $a_l - D[l+1][r]$; the same on the right; the maximum is the new value. The first player's total is $(S + D)/2$.

**C++ details explained.** `vector<long long> diff(a)` copies `a`: for length 1 the difference is the only number. `for (auto& x : a) cin >> x, sum += x;` uses the comma operator to read and add in one statement. $S + D$ is always even ($S + D = 2 \cdot$ the first total), so the division is exact even for negative values.

**Tested.** Stress-tested against a plain minimax recursion that returns both totals, on 5000 random inputs ($n \le 14$, values up to 9 or $10^9$, sometimes negative). There are also 5 fixed tests: the example of Section 4.3 (11), one negative number ($-7$), the row $1, 5, 2, 1$ (6), 5000 random values in $[-10^9, 10^9]$ (from a separate program), and 5000 equal values ($2.5 \cdot 10^{12}$).

**Alternative formulation.** Store the first player's best total directly: $F[l][r] = \max(a_l + (S(l+1, r) - F[l+1][r]), \ a_r + (S(l, r-1) - F[l][r-1]))$, since the mover gets what the opponent does not. It needs segment sums from prefix sums; the difference form avoids them.

### Longest palindromic subsequence (Theorem 3.6.4)

<Code code={palindromeSubsequence} lang="cpp" title="palindrome-subsequence/solution.cpp" />

**How it works.** The rows of the table $P[l][r]$ are filled for $l$ from $n - 1$ down to 0, and within a row for $r$ from $l$ upwards. $P[l][r]$ needs $P[l+1][r-1]$ and $P[l+1][r]$ (the previous row, `next`) and $P[l][r-1]$ (the same row, already filled). Equal ends add 2 to the inside; different ends drop one end (Theorem 3.6.4). Two rows of $n$ ints replace the $n^2$ table: $2.5 \cdot 10^7$ steps for $n = 5000$.

**C++ details explained.** `swap(next, cur)` exchanges the two vectors in $O(1)$; after the last swap, `next` holds row 0. The inner segment of $[l, l + 1]$ is empty, which the test `l + 1 <= r - 1` handles. Stale values in `cur` from two rows ago are never read: each entry `cur[r]` with $r \ge l$ is written before it is used.

**Tested.** Stress-tested against a brute force that checks every subsequence, on 5000 random strings (length $\le 14$, 1–4 letters). There are also 5 fixed tests: "abcab" (3), one letter (1), 5000 equal letters (5000), "abab…ab" of length 5000 (4999), and a random string of length 3000 over three letters (from a separate program).

**Alternative formulation.** The longest palindromic subsequence of $s$ is the LCS of $s$ and its reverse (topic 3.4), with the same $O(n^2)$ cost. The segment table is more direct, and its rows are what the palindrome problems of the ladder build on.

### Longest correct bracket subsequence (Theorem 3.6.5)

<Code code={bracketPairs} lang="cpp" title="bracket-pairs/solution.cpp" />

**How it works.** Look at the first bracket $s_l$ of the segment. Either it is not used, and the answer is that of $[l + 1, r]$; or it is matched with a closing bracket $s_k$ of the same type. Then everything used strictly between them must be matched among itself (brackets cannot cross), and everything after $s_k$ likewise: $2 + \text{best}[l+1][k-1] + \text{best}[k+1][r]$. This is the "first element and its partner" pattern of Theorem 3.6.5: fixing the partner splits the rest into an inside and an outside that do not interact. Time $O(n^3)$.

**C++ details explained.** The lambda `get` returns 0 for empty segments ($l > r$), which removes the special cases for $k = l + 1$ and $k = r$. `matches` keeps the bracket types in one place; a closing bracket at position $l$ is simply never matched as an opener.

**Tested.** Stress-tested against a brute force that checks every subsequence with a stack, on 5000 random strings (length $\le 14$, one or two bracket types). There are also 5 fixed tests: "([)]" (2: crossing pairs are not allowed), a single "(" (0), "([])[]" (6), 250 opening followed by 250 closing brackets (500), and a random string of length 300 (from a separate program).

**Alternative formulation.** Match the last element instead: either $s_r$ is unused, or it closes some opener $s_k$ with $k < r$, giving $\text{best}[l][k-1] + 2 + \text{best}[k+1][r-1]$. Any fixed end works, as long as one end is always the one being matched.

## 7. Bug catalogue

Each bug below was made by changing a template from Section 6 and caught by `scripts/stress.sh`. The input shown is the shortest failing input among the first 2000 random seeds. Three of the six are about the order in which the table is filled or read, the most common interval-DP mistake.

### Bug: filling by the left end

Template: cheapest bracketing.

```cpp
for (int l = 1; l <= n; l++) {
    for (int r = l + 1; r <= n; r++) {
```

Input: `3`, then the sizes `2 5 3 3`.

Wrong output `30`, correct output `48`. With $l = 1$ first, `best[1][3]` is computed before `best[2][3]`, which still holds 0: the split $k = 1$ looks free apart from its last multiplication, $2 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 = 30$.

**Fix:** loop over the length outside (or over $l$ downwards), so that every segment is computed after the shorter segments inside it (Theorem 3.6.1, note 1).

### Bug: overwriting a row in the wrong direction

Template: taking from the ends.

```cpp
for (int l = n - len; l >= 0; l--) {
```

Input: `3`, then `2 1 8`.

Wrong output `10`, correct output `9` (take 8, the opponent takes 2, you get 1). With one array per length, `diff[l + 1]` must still hold the shorter segment $[l + 1, r]$ when `diff[l]` is computed. Going from right to left, `diff[l + 1]` has already been replaced by the longer segment $[l + 1, r + 1]$.

**Fix:** when one array stores the values of the previous length in place, update it in the direction that reads old entries before they are overwritten: here $l$ increasing. Or keep two arrays.

### Bug: adding the opponent's difference

Template: taking from the ends.

```cpp
diff[l] = max(a[l] + diff[l + 1], a[r] + diff[l]);
```

Input: `2`, then `5 6`.

Wrong output `11`, correct output `6`. `diff` is the advantage of the player to move; after your move the opponent moves, so their advantage counts against you.

**Fix:** `a[l] − diff[l + 1]` (Theorem 3.6.3). When the state is "the player to move", every recursive value changes sign once per move.

### Bug: dropping both ends on a mismatch

Template: longest palindromic subsequence.

```cpp
else cur[r] = (l + 1 <= r - 1 ? next[r - 1] : 0);
```

Input: `ba`.

Wrong output `0`, correct output `1`. When the ends differ, only one of them must go; dropping both loses palindromes that use one end, here the single letter b (or a).

**Fix:** on a mismatch take the larger of $[l + 1, r]$ and $[l, r - 1]$ (Theorem 3.6.4), as in the LCS table of topic 3.4.

### Bug: forgetting the part after the partner

Template: longest correct bracket subsequence.

```cpp
if (matches(s[l], s[k])) value = max(value, 2 + get(l + 1, k - 1));
```

Input: `()()`.

Wrong output `2`, correct output `4`. Matching $s_l$ with $s_k$ splits the segment into the inside $(l, k)$ **and** the outside $[k + 1, r]$; the outside can still hold matched pairs.

**Fix:** add `get(k + 1, r)` (Theorem 3.6.5). After fixing the partner, list every part the segment falls into and make sure each one is counted.

### Bug: any closing bracket as a partner

Template: longest correct bracket subsequence.

```cpp
if ((s[l] == '(' || s[l] == '[') && (s[k] == ')' || s[k] == ']')) value = ...
```

Input: `(]`.

Wrong output `2`, correct output `0`. A partner must have the matching type; the pairing is allowed only when the two elements really go together.

**Fix:** test the pair with `matches`. Test with two bracket types and crossing pairs such as "([)]".

## 8. Worked examples

### Example 1: Slimes

[AtCoder Educational DP Contest (EDPC), problem N: Slimes](https://atcoder.jp/contests/dp/tasks/dp_n). In short: $N \le 400$ slimes in a row with sizes up to $10^9$; combining two neighbours of sizes $x$ and $y$ gives one slime of size $x + y$ and costs $x + y$. Find the least total cost of combining all of them.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$N = 400$: $O(N^3) \approx 400^3 / 6 \approx 10^7$ steps is fast. The costs reach $400 \cdot 400 \cdot 10^9$, so `long long`.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Trying every order of combinations (topic 3.1): $(N - 1)!$ orders.
- Greedy "combine the cheapest neighbouring pair" (topic 1.5): fails, as Section 1 shows with $(2, 2, 1, 2)$.
- Huffman's greedy with a heap (topic 0.6): only valid when any two slimes may be combined, not just neighbours.
- The best split point (Theorem 3.6.2): the merge-split card.

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The card needs a last event that splits the segment into independent parts. The last combination joins one slime made from slimes $1..k$ with one made from $k + 1..N$: only neighbours are ever combined, so each side is a contiguous block, and the two blocks never interacted. Its cost is the total size of the row, whatever $k$ is. Decision: $\text{best}[l][r] = \min_k (\text{best}[l][k] + \text{best}[k+1][r]) + S(l, r)$, filled by length.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

This is Theorem 3.6.2 word for word, with slimes as piles: the last combination of $[l, r]$ costs $S(l, r) = $ prefix$[r] - $ prefix$[l - 1]$ for every split point, and the two sides are combined independently, each at least at its own best cost, which is achievable. The table is filled by increasing length (Theorem 3.6.1), so both sides are known when $[l, r]$ is computed.

#### Step 6: Alternative approach

Memoised recursion over $(l, r)$ (Theorem 3.2.4) gives the same $O(N^3)$. There is also a faster $O(N^2)$ method for this exact cost (Knuth's optimisation, which uses the fact that the best split point moves monotonically), but it needs a proof outside this course; at $N = 400$ the cubic table is enough.

### Example 2: Palindrome Partitioning II

[LeetCode 132: Palindrome Partitioning II](https://leetcode.com/problems/palindrome-partitioning-ii/). In short: cut a string of length up to 2000 into pieces that are all palindromes, using as few cuts as possible.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$n = 2000$: $O(n^2) = 4 \cdot 10^6$ fits; $O(n^3) = 8 \cdot 10^9$ does not.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Trying every set of cuts (topic 0.5): $2^{1999}$ sets.
- Greedy "take the longest palindrome from the left" (topic 1.5): fails. For "abaab" the longest palindrome at the start is "aba", leaving "a" and "b" (2 cuts), while "a" + "baab" needs only 1.
- A segment table with a split point (Theorem 3.6.2): correct but $O(n^3)$.
- The palindrome table of Section 4.4, followed by a one-index table over prefixes (topic 3.2): the palindrome-segment card.

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The card asks whether the answer depends on palindromes inside segments: here every piece must be a palindrome, and whether $s_l..s_r$ is one depends only on $s_l = s_r$ and the inner segment. So $\text{pal}[l][r]$ is an $O(n^2)$ table. The cuts themselves are a sequence from left to right: the last piece of a prefix is some palindrome $s_j..s_{i-1}$. Decision: the palindrome table, then $\text{pieces}[i] = \min_{j:\ \text{pal}[j][i-1]} \text{pieces}[j] + 1$, both $O(n^2)$.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

$\text{pal}[l][r]$ is correct by induction on the length: a string of length at least 2 is a palindrome exactly when its ends are equal and the inside is a palindrome, and lengths 0 and 1 are palindromes. For the pieces: a partition of the prefix of length $i$ into palindromes ends with one piece $s_j..s_{i-1}$, which must be a palindrome, and the rest is a partition of the prefix of length $j$; every such combination is valid. So the fewest pieces is the minimum over valid $j$ of $\text{pieces}[j] + 1$ (Theorem 3.2.1), and the answer is that number minus one, since $k$ pieces need $k - 1$ cuts.
