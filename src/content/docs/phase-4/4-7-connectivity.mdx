## 1. Why this topic exists

Here are two small problems. A city has $n = 2 \cdot 10^5$ intersections and $m = 2 \cdot 10^5$ **one-way** streets. The mayor asks: "can a car drive from any intersection to any other?", and if not, "which groups of intersections are such that inside a group every intersection can be reached from every other?" Next, a network of $n$ servers is joined by $m$ **two-way** cables. An engineer asks: "which single cable, if it breaks, splits the network into two parts?" and "which single server, if it fails, does the same?"

The tools of [topic 4.1](../4-1-graph-traversal/) and [topic 4.2](../4-2-graph-structure/) answer one question about one vertex: a search from a vertex finds all vertices it reaches. To find the groups, a search from every vertex costs $O(n(n+m))$, about $10^{11}$ steps. For the cables, removing each cable and searching again costs $O(m(n+m))$, as much. We need one pass over the graph that answers the question for all vertices or all edges at once.

The key idea is one depth-first walk with two numbers per vertex. The **entry time** $\text{tin}[v]$ is the moment the walk first reaches $v$. In an undirected graph, every edge that the walk does not use joins a vertex to one of its ancestors in the walk tree. So the part of the graph below a vertex $v$ is tied to the rest only by the tree edge above $v$ and by such upward edges; the **low-link value** $\text{low}[v]$ is the smallest entry time that the part below $v$ can reach with one upward edge. If it is not smaller than $\text{tin}[v]$, the part below is cut off from the rest when the edge above is removed: that edge is a **bridge**. The same numbers, with a slightly different comparison, find the **articulation points**. For one-way streets, a second walk on the reversed streets, started in the order the first walk finished, peels off the groups one by one (Kosaraju's method): each group is a **strongly connected component**, and the groups themselves form a graph with no cycle, which can be reasoned about like the DAGs of [topic 4.2](../4-2-graph-structure/).

## 3. Definitions

- In a directed graph, vertices $u$ and $v$ are **strongly connected** if $v$ is reachable from $u$ and $u$ is reachable from $v$ ([topic 4.1](../4-1-graph-traversal/)). A **strongly connected component** (SCC) is a largest set of vertices that are pairwise strongly connected. Every vertex lies in exactly one component (Theorem 4.7.5), and a vertex with no cycle through it is a component of its own.
- The **condensation** of a directed graph has one vertex per strongly connected component and an edge from component $A$ to component $B$ when some edge of the graph goes from a vertex of $A$ to a vertex of $B$. A **source** component has no edge entering it and a **sink** component has no edge leaving it.
- In an undirected graph, a **bridge** is an edge whose removal increases the number of connected components. An **articulation point** (cut vertex) is a vertex whose removal, with its edges, increases the number of connected components.
- A **2-edge-connected component** is a set of vertices that stay connected to each other after the removal of any single edge; the components are the pieces left when all bridges are removed.
- In a depth-first walk of an undirected graph the **entry time** $\text{tin}[v]$ numbers the vertices $0, 1, 2, \dots$ in the order the walk first reaches them (as in the Euler tour of [topic 4.5](../4-5-trees/)). The edges by which the walk reaches new vertices are the **tree edges**; every other edge is a **back edge**. The **low-link value** $\text{low}[v]$ is the smallest $\text{tin}$ of a vertex that can be reached from the subtree of $v$ using tree edges downward and at most one back edge (Theorem 4.7.1).
- The code units number the vertices from 1 and give an edge its own number, so that two roads between the same towns are different edges. A graph may have several edges between two vertices; self-loops do not matter for any question here.

## 4. Theory and proofs

### 4.1 The depth-first walk with entry times and low-link values

**Theorem 4.7.1 (entry times and low-link values).** Let $G$ be an undirected graph; parallel edges are allowed and every edge has its own number. Walk it depth first: start at an unvisited vertex, and at a vertex $v$ look at the edges of $v$ one by one except the edge by which $v$ was reached. An edge to an unvisited vertex $u$ is a *tree edge*: the walk moves to $u$. An edge to an already visited vertex is a *back edge*. Let $\text{tin}[v]$ be the number of $v$ in the order of first visits ($0, 1, 2, \dots$ over all walks). Then:

(a) every back edge joins a vertex with one of its ancestors in the walk forest, never a vertex with a vertex in a different branch;

(b) for every $v$ let $\text{low}[v]$ be the smallest of $\text{tin}[v]$ and of the numbers $\text{tin}[w]$ over all back edges $\{x, w\}$ with $x$ in the subtree of $v$. Then $\text{low}[v]$ is the smallest of $\text{tin}[v]$, of $\text{tin}[w]$ for the back edges $\{v, w\}$ at $v$, and of $\text{low}[c]$ for the children $c$ of $v$;

(c) the walk computes all $\text{tin}$ and $\text{low}$ in $O(n + m)$ time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Walk the graph depth first and number the vertices in the order you meet them. Every edge you do not walk along leads from a vertex up to one of its ancestors, never sideways. For each vertex, $\text{low}$ is the earliest-numbered place that the part of the walk below the vertex can reach by one such upward edge.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The triangle $1\!-\!2\!-\!3\!-\!1$, the edge $3\!-\!4$ and two edges between 4 and 5. The walk from 1 meets $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ with $\text{tin} = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$. The back edges are $3\!-\!1$ (from 3 up to 1) and the second edge between 5 and 4. So $\text{low} = 0, 0, 0, 3, 3$: the part below vertex 4 reaches only the places numbered 3 and 4, while the part below vertex 2 reaches vertex 1 (number 0).

**Proof.**
1. *Claim (a).* Let $\{a, b\}$ be a back edge with $\text{tin}[a] < \text{tin}[b]$. When the walk enters $a$, vertex $b$ has not been visited.
2. The walk leaves $a$ only after it has looked at all edges of $a$, among them this one. At that moment $b$ must be visited, since an edge to an unvisited vertex becomes a tree edge and this one is not.
3. So $b$ was entered after $a$ was entered and before $a$ was left. The vertices entered in that time are exactly the descendants of $a$, so $a$ is an ancestor of $b$.
4. *Claim (b).* The back edges that start in the subtree of $v$ either start at $v$ itself or in the subtree of one child $c$; taking the smallest of the three kinds of numbers is the same as taking the smallest over all of them.
5. *Claim (c).* The walk sets $\text{low}[v] = \text{tin}[v]$ when it enters $v$, lowers it to $\text{tin}[w]$ at each back edge to $w$, and lowers it to $\text{low}[c]$ when it leaves a child $c$. By (b) this gives $\text{low}[v]$. Every edge is looked at twice and every vertex is left once, so the time is $O(n + m)$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The edge by which the walk came is skipped by its **number**, not by the vertex at its other end. A second edge to the parent is a back edge, and it makes $\text{low}$ smaller (it is the reason the double edge $4\!-\!5$ above does not count as a bridge in Theorem 4.7.2).
2. An explicit stack, as in [topic 4.5](../4-5-trees/): a chain of $2 \cdot 10^5$ vertices would overflow a recursive walk on some judges.
3. A graph with several pieces needs one walk per piece, so the loop over the start vertices is part of the algorithm.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.7/thm-1" />

The first example is the tiny instance (numbers $\text{tin}$ and $\text{low}$ as above), the second is a graph of two pieces, where the second walk continues the numbering at 3. The check compares every vertex of twenty thousand random graphs, with double edges, with the definition of $\text{low}$ and tests statement (a) on every back edge.

### 4.2 Bridges

**Theorem 4.7.2 (bridges).** With the numbers of Theorem 4.7.1, a back edge is never a bridge, and the tree edge from a vertex $v$ to its parent $p$ is a bridge exactly when $\text{low}[v] > \text{tin}[p]$. All bridges are found in $O(n + m)$ time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Look at the part of the walk below $v$. If something in it has an edge going up past $v$'s parent (or to the parent itself, through another edge), cutting the edge to the parent does not separate it. If the part below can reach only itself, then the edge to the parent is the only link, and cutting it cuts the part off.
:::

**A tiny instance.** In the instance of Theorem 4.7.1, the tree edge $3\!-\!4$ has $\text{low}[4] = 3 > \text{tin}[3] = 2$: a bridge. The edge $2\!-\!3$ has $\text{low}[3] = 0 \le \text{tin}[2] = 1$ (the back edge $3\!-\!1$ goes over it): not a bridge. The tree edge $4\!-\!5$ has $\text{low}[5] = 3 \le \text{tin}[4] = 3$ (the second edge to 4): not a bridge.

**Proof.**
1. *A back edge.* A back edge $\{a, b\}$ with $b$ below $a$ closes a cycle with the tree path from $a$ to $b$ (Theorem 4.7.1 (a)). Its removal leaves $a$ and $b$ joined by the tree path, so it is not a bridge.
2. *A tree edge.* Let $p$ be the parent of $v$ and $S$ the subtree of $v$. Without the edge $p\!-\!v$, the vertices of $S$ are still joined by tree edges, and the rest of the piece of the graph is still joined by tree edges, too. So the piece falls apart exactly when no other edge joins $S$ to the outside of $S$.
3. Another edge leaving $S$ is a back edge $\{x, w\}$ with $x$ in $S$ and $w$ outside. By Theorem 4.7.1 (a), $w$ is an ancestor of $x$ (a descendant of $x$ would be in $S$), so $w$ is a proper ancestor of $v$: $\text{tin}[w] \le \text{tin}[p]$.
4. If such an edge exists, $\text{low}[v] \le \text{tin}[w] \le \text{tin}[p]$. Conversely, if $\text{low}[v] \le \text{tin}[p]$, then $\text{low}[v] < \text{tin}[v]$ is the number $\text{tin}[w]$ of a back edge $\{x, w\}$ with $x$ in $S$; every vertex of $S$ has a number of at least $\text{tin}[v]$, so $w$ is outside $S$ and the edge leaves $S$.
5. So the tree edge is a bridge exactly when $\text{low}[v] > \text{tin}[p]$. The numbers come from one walk. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Step 3 uses Theorem 4.7.1 (a): an edge from the subtree of $v$ to a vertex that is not an ancestor does not exist.
2. A second edge between $v$ and its parent has another number, so it is a back edge with $\text{tin}[w] = \text{tin}[p]$ and makes $\text{low}[v] \le \text{tin}[p]$: the pair of edges is not a bridge.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.7/thm-2" />

The examples are the tiny instance (only edge 3, the edge $3\!-\!4$, is a bridge), a square with a diagonal (none) and a tree (all four edges are bridges). The check removes each edge of twenty thousand random graphs and counts the pieces.

### 4.3 Articulation points

**Theorem 4.7.3 (articulation points).** With the numbers of Theorem 4.7.1, a vertex $v$ that is not the first vertex of its walk is an articulation point exactly when some child $c$ of $v$ has $\text{low}[c] \ge \text{tin}[v]$. The first vertex of a walk (the root) is an articulation point exactly when it has at least two children. All are found in $O(n + m)$ time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Take away $v$ and see what happens to each branch hanging below it. A branch survives only if it has an edge that jumps over $v$ to somewhere above. If one branch has no such edge, it is cut off. The root has nothing above it, so there it is different: it is a cut vertex only when the walk has to start two or more separate branches from it.
:::

**A tiny instance.** In the instance of Theorem 4.7.1: vertex 3 has the child 4 with $\text{low}[4] = 3 \ge \text{tin}[3] = 2$, so vertex 3 is an articulation point. Vertex 4 has the child 5 with $\text{low}[5] = 3 \ge \text{tin}[4] = 3$: also one (the double edge does not help, since it goes to 4 itself). Vertex 2 has the child 3 with $\text{low}[3] = 0 < 1$: not one. Vertex 1 is the root with one child: not one.

**Proof.**
1. *A vertex $v$ that is not a root.* Remove $v$. The vertices outside the subtree of $v$ stay joined by tree edges (and the parent of $v$ is among them). Each subtree $S_c$ of a child $c$ is joined inside by tree edges.
2. By Theorem 4.7.1 (a), the only edges leaving $S_c$ are the tree edge $v\!-\!c$ and back edges to proper ancestors of $c$, that is, to $v$ or to proper ancestors of $v$. There are no edges between two different child subtrees.
3. After the removal of $v$, the subtree $S_c$ is still tied to the rest exactly when it has a back edge to a proper ancestor of $v$. As in the proof of Theorem 4.7.2, this means $\text{low}[c] < \text{tin}[v]$.
4. If every child satisfies this, everything stays together. If some child has $\text{low}[c] \ge \text{tin}[v]$, then $S_c$ is cut off from the rest, and the rest is not empty (it contains the parent of $v$): one more piece.
5. *A root $r$.* Removing $r$ leaves its child subtrees, which are joined inside, and no edge goes between two of them (step 2). So the number of pieces equals the number of children. It is larger than before exactly when there are at least two children (with one child the piece stays one piece, and with none the vertex was alone). $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The root is treated separately. For the root, $\text{low}[c] \ge \text{tin}[r] = 0$ is always true, so the rule for other vertices would mark every root with a child.
2. The test is "$\ge$" here and "$>$" for bridges: the back edge to $v$ itself does not save a branch when $v$ is removed, but it does save it when only the edge above is removed.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.7/thm-3" />

The examples are the tiny instance (3 and 4), a star walked from its centre (only the centre) and a square (none). The check removes each vertex of twenty thousand random graphs and counts the pieces.

### 4.4 Two-edge-connected pieces and one-way streets

**Theorem 4.7.4 (pieces without bridges).** Let $G$ be an undirected graph and cut the walk forest of Theorem 4.7.1 at the bridges.

(a) The pieces of the walk forest are exactly the connected pieces of $G$ without its bridges, that is, the 2-edge-connected components. Walking through the vertices in the order of entry, a vertex gets the label of its parent if the edge to the parent is not a bridge, and a new label otherwise.

(b) Contract every piece to one vertex. The edges that remain are the bridges, and they form a forest (a tree if $G$ is connected).

(c) If $G$ is connected and has no bridge, orient every tree edge from the parent to the child and every back edge from the lower vertex to its ancestor. The result is strongly connected: from every vertex one can reach every other vertex.

:::tip[In plain words]
The bridges are the only roads whose loss splits the network, so cutting them leaves pieces that hold together strongly, and the pieces hang together as a tree. If there is no bridge at all, you can make every street one-way and still drive from anywhere to anywhere: drive down along the tree and back up along the extra edges.
:::

**A tiny instance.** Two triangles $1\!-\!2\!-\!3$ and $4\!-\!5\!-\!6$ joined by the edge $3\!-\!4$. The only bridge is $3\!-\!4$, so there are two pieces, $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and $\{4, 5, 6\}$, and the contracted graph is one edge between two vertices. A lone triangle oriented by (c): the tree edges $1\!\to\!2$ and $2\!\to\!3$ and the back edge $3\!\to\!1$ give the cycle.

**Proof.**
1. *Claim (a).* Both ends of a back edge lie in the same piece. Indeed, the edge closes a cycle with the tree path between its ends, so all the tree edges on that path lie on a cycle and none is a bridge (Theorem 4.7.2). Hence after the bridges are cut, connections between pieces cannot go along a back edge, so the connected pieces of $G$ without its bridges are the pieces of the tree.
2. Two vertices in one piece stay joined when one edge is removed: they are joined by a path without bridges, and removing a single non-bridge edge does not change the pieces of the graph. Two vertices in different pieces are separated by a bridge on their tree path.
3. *Claim (b).* A cycle in the contracted graph would give a cycle of $G$ through bridges, and an edge on a cycle is not a bridge. So the contracted graph has no cycle; it has one tree per connected piece of $G$.
4. *Claim (c).* The root reaches every vertex along the tree edges downwards.
5. Let $v$ be a vertex other than the root and $p$ its parent. As there is no bridge, $\text{low}[v] \le \text{tin}[p] < \text{tin}[v]$, so there is a back edge $\{x, w\}$ with $x$ in the subtree of $v$ and $w$ a proper ancestor of $v$ (Theorem 4.7.2, step 4). From $v$ go down the tree edges to $x$ and then up along the back edge to $w$.
6. Repeating step 5 climbs to the root in finitely many rounds, since each round reaches a vertex with a smaller number. So every vertex reaches the root, and the root reaches every vertex. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The vertices must be taken in the order of entry, so that the label of the parent is known before the child.
2. Part (c) needs a connected graph without bridges: with a bridge, one direction of that edge would cut off one side.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.7/thm-4" />

The first example is the tiny instance with two triangles, the second the lone triangle, the third a square with a diagonal and a double edge, whose orientation is still a cycle through all four vertices. The check compares the pieces with the pieces found by removing every edge of thirty thousand random graphs, and tests strong connectivity of the orientation on every random graph that is connected and has no bridge.

### 4.5 Strongly connected components

**Theorem 4.7.5 (Kosaraju's method).** Let $G$ be a directed graph. "$u$ and $v$ reach each other" is an equivalence relation; its classes are the strongly connected components, and each vertex lies in exactly one. Do a depth-first walk of $G$ (as often as needed to cover all vertices) and list the vertices in the order they are *left*. Then go through this list from the last to the first and, for each vertex not yet seen, walk the **reversed** graph from it (never entering seen vertices). The vertices of each such walk form one strongly connected component, and the components are found in an order in which every edge between two components goes from a component found earlier to one found later. The time is $O(n + m)$.

:::tip[In plain words]
The first pass puts the vertices in a list so that the vertex left last sits in a group nobody else points into. Take the groups from there: from that vertex, walk the arrows **backwards**. You can only reach the vertices that can reach it, and the only unseen ones among them are in its own group. Take that group away and continue with the next vertex of the list.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The edges $1\!\to\!2$, $2\!\to\!3$, $3\!\to\!1$, $3\!\to\!4$, $4\!\leftrightarrow\!5$ and $6\!\to\!1$. Pass 1 from 1 leaves the vertices in the order $5, 4, 3, 2, 1$ and then, from 6, leaves 6. The list from the last: 6 first. On the reversed graph nothing enters 6, so $\{6\}$ is the first component. Next, 1: the reversed walk finds 3 and 2 ($\{1, 2, 3\}$). Next, 4: finds 5 ($\{4, 5\}$). The components $\{6\}, \{1, 2, 3\}, \{4, 5\}$ are found in the order of the edges $6\!\to\!1$ and $3\!\to\!4$.

**Proof.**
1. *The relation.* It is reflexive (the path of length zero), symmetric by definition, and transitive: join the path from $u$ to $v$ with the path from $v$ to $w$, and the same backwards. So the vertices split into classes.
2. *A lemma.* Let $A \ne B$ be components with an edge from $A$ to $B$. Then the vertex of $A$ that is left last is left after every vertex of $B$.
3. Look at the first vertex $x$ of $A \cup B$ that pass 1 enters. If $x \in A$: every vertex of $A \cup B$ is reachable from $x$ and not yet visited, so all of them are entered while $x$ is active and are left before $x$. If $x \in B$: no vertex of $A$ is reachable from $x$ (otherwise $A$ and $B$ would be one component), so all of $B$ is left before the first vertex of $A$ is even entered.
4. *Pass 2.* Let $s$ be the unseen vertex that was left last and $C$ its component. No unseen component has an edge into $C$: such a component would contain a vertex that was left after $s$ by the lemma, and it is unseen, contradicting the choice of $s$.
5. The reversed walk from $s$ finds the unseen vertices from which $s$ can be reached through unseen vertices. If it found a vertex outside $C$, the path from it to $s$ would have a last edge entering $C$ from an unseen vertex of another component, which step 4 forbids. All of $C$ is found, since the vertices of $C$ reach $s$ inside $C$. So the walk collects exactly $C$.
6. A component found now has no edge entering it from an unseen component, so every edge between two components goes from an earlier one to a later one. The time is two walks. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The reversed graph is built once, in $O(n + m)$, and the second pass never enters a vertex that is already in a component.
2. Both passes use an explicit stack with a counter of the edges looked at for each vertex, as for the walk of Theorem 4.7.1, so a chain of $2 \cdot 10^5$ vertices is safe.
3. Components get the numbers $1, 2, \dots$ in the order they are found. This order is a topological order of the condensation, which Theorem 4.7.6 uses.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.7/thm-5" />

The first example is the tiny instance (the labels are 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 1 for the vertices 1 to 6: the vertex 6 is found first, then $\{1, 2, 3\}$, then $\{4, 5\}$). The second is a path, where each vertex is its own component. The check compares the groups of thirty thousand random graphs with the reachability table, and tests that every edge goes from a smaller to a larger or equal label.

### 4.6 The graph of components

**Theorem 4.7.6 (the condensation).** Let the components of a directed graph be numbered as in Theorem 4.7.5.

(a) Every edge of $G$ goes from a vertex of a smaller or the same component number to a vertex of a larger or the same number. So the condensation has no directed cycle; it is a DAG as in [topic 4.2](../4-2-graph-structure/), and the numbers are a topological order.

(b) A vertex $s$ reaches every vertex of $G$ exactly when its component is the **only** source of the condensation (the only component that no edge enters). This is checked in $O(n + m)$ time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Squeeze every group into one dot: what is left is a map of one-way roads without a cycle. In such a map, a dot that nothing points into is a starting dot, and you can drive from a starting dot to everything exactly when it is the only starting dot, since every other dot is reached from some starting dot.
:::

**A tiny instance.** In the instance of Theorem 4.7.5 the condensation has the edges $\{6\} \to \{1,2,3\} \to \{4,5\}$; the only source is $\{6\}$. So vertex 6 reaches every vertex, while vertex 1 does not (it cannot reach 6).

**Proof.**
1. *Claim (a).* By step 6 of the proof of Theorem 4.7.5, every edge between two different components goes from an earlier-found component to a later-found one, that is, from a smaller to a larger number. A directed cycle of components would need a step back to a smaller number somewhere.
2. *Claim (b), "only if".* Suppose $s$ reaches everything. A source $D$ other than the component of $s$ cannot be reached from $s$: a path into $D$ would use an edge entering $D$, and there is none. So the component of $s$ is the only source or no source exists, and a DAG has at least one source (the component with the smallest number).
3. Suppose the component of $s$ is not a source: some edge enters it. Go backwards along entering edges; the numbers decrease, so this ends at a source $D$, and $D$ differs from the component of $s$. By the previous step $s$ cannot reach $D$.
4. *"If".* Suppose the component of $s$ is the only source. For any component $C$ go backwards along entering edges until a source is reached; this is the component of $s$. Following the edges forwards, together with the paths inside the components, gives a path from $s$ to every vertex of $C$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. A graph with no edge between components has every component as a source; then the only-source test fails unless there is a single component.
2. The test looks at edges between different components only; edges inside a component do not make it a non-source.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.7/thm-6" />

The examples are a diamond ($1\!\to\!2$, $1\!\to\!3$, $2\!\to\!4$, $3\!\to\!4$, where vertex 1 reaches everything and vertex 2 does not) and a pair $1 \leftrightarrow 2$ with an edge $3\!\to\!1$ (vertex 3 reaches everything, vertex 1 does not). The check compares the answer for every start vertex of thirty thousand random graphs with the reachability table.

## 6. Templates

Eight templates. Each shows only the function that matters; the program around it reads the input. The functions use `LowLink` of Theorem 4.7.1 (for two-way graphs), `edgeComponents` and `orientEdges` of Theorem 4.7.4, and `stronglyConnected` of Theorem 4.7.5 (for one-way graphs), and say so. **How it works.** `stronglyConnected` gives every group a number in the order in which the second walk finds the groups. That order depends on the walks, so the function renames the groups by the first town in each, and the answer is the same for any correct method. The number of kingdoms is `count`.

### Everyone to everyone (Theorems 4.7.5 and 4.7.6)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.7/everyone-to-everyone" />

**How it works.** If there is only one group, every town reaches every town. Otherwise the numbering has every road going from a smaller to a larger number, so a town of the last group cannot reach a town of the first group, and the pair is a witness. The program around the function checks the witness with a plain search, so a wrong pair would be noticed.

**Alternative formulation.** One search from town 1 on the roads and one on the reversed roads also decide the question: the graph is one group exactly when both searches reach every town. The groups are needed only when the witness or the groups themselves are asked for.

### Fewest guides (Theorems 4.7.5 and 4.7.6)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.7/fewest-guides" />

**How it works.** A group that no line enters from another group has to contain a guide, because nothing else reaches it. One guide in each such group reaches every town: every group is reached from some source by going backwards along entering lines (Theorem 4.7.6). So the answer is the number of groups without an entering line.

**Alternative formulation.** If a town is also given and the question is how many lines to add so that the town reaches all, count the sources other than the group of that town (and add nothing if its group is the only source).

### Roads on every route (Theorem 4.7.2)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.7/roads-on-every-route" />

**How it works.** The walk starts at town 1. A bridge from a town to its parent separates the subtree below from the rest; the target town is cut off from town 1 exactly when it is in that subtree, which is a test on the entry times: $\text{tin}[v] \le \text{tin}[\text{target}] < \text{tin}[v] + \text{size}[v]$. The sizes come from adding up over the visit order backwards, as in [topic 4.5](../4-5-trees/).

**Alternative formulation.** To list the roads instead of counting, keep their numbers (`parentEdge[v]`).

### Pieces after a failure (Theorem 4.7.3)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.7/pieces-after-failure" />

**How it works.** For a town that is not the first of the walk, the part outside its subtree is one piece (it contains the parent), and every child $c$ with $\text{low}[c] \ge \text{tin}[v]$ is another piece, by the proof of Theorem 4.7.3. For the first town, every child subtree is a piece. A town with no neighbours has no pieces to count. The town is an articulation point exactly when the number is larger than 1.

**Alternative formulation.** For several separate pieces of the graph, add the other pieces to every count, since removing a town does not touch them.

### Towns cut off by a closed road (Theorem 4.7.2)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.7/cut-off-towns" />

**How it works.** Closing a bridge cuts off its lower part from town 1; the size of the lower part is the size of the subtree below the bridge. Closing any other road cuts off nothing, and the answer for that road stays 0. The answer is stored by road number, so the output is in the input order.

**Alternative formulation.** The number of pairs of towns that can no longer reach each other after closing the bridge is $s \cdot (n - s)$ with $s$ the size of the lower part; the number of pairs that stay connected is $\binom{s}{2} + \binom{n-s}{2}$.

### Groups that survive a closed road (Theorem 4.7.4)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.7/road-groups" />

**How it works.** `edgeComponents` labels the pieces of the walk tree after the bridges are cut. Counting the labels gives the size of every group, and sorting makes the answer independent of the labels. A graph without bridges is one group.

**Alternative formulation.** To work on the tree of groups, the bridges are its edges: the road $p\!-\!v$ of a bridge joins the group of $p$ to the group of $v$. The number of leaves of this tree is the number of groups with exactly one bridge.

### One-way streets (Theorem 4.7.4)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.7/one-way-streets" />

**How it works.** All streets can be made one-way, keeping every town reachable from every other, exactly when no street is a bridge (the connected graph case of Theorem 4.7.4 (c)). The program around the function builds the orientation (`orientEdges`) and checks it with two searches from town 1, one on the streets and one on the reversed streets, so a wrong answer would be noticed.

**Alternative formulation.** If the graph may fall into several pieces, apply the test to every piece. If only some streets may be directed, the others need two-way travel and the question changes.

## 7. Bug catalogue

Each bug below was made by changing a template from Section 6 (or the walk of Theorem 4.7.1 that it uses) and caught by `scripts/stress.sh`. The inputs shown are small failing inputs, checked against the brute force.

### Bug: skipping the parent by the town instead of the road

Template: towns cut off by a closed road (the walk of Theorem 4.7.1).

```cpp
if (to == parent[v]) continue;                         // instead of: if (id == parentEdge[v]) continue;
```

Input: `2 2`, then the roads `2 1` and `1 2` (two parallel roads between the two towns).

Wrong output `1 0`, correct output `0 0`. Closing either of the two roads leaves the other, so nothing is cut off. The wrong walk treats the second road as the road it came by and ignores it, so the first road looks like a bridge. Parallel roads are different roads, and only the road by number is the one to skip (Theorem 4.7.1, note 1).

**Fix:** skip the edge whose number is `parentEdge[v]`.

### Bug: "at least" in the test for a bridge

Template: towns cut off by a closed road.

```cpp
if (L.low[v] >= L.tin[L.parent[v]]) cutOff[L.parentEdge[v]] = size[v];   // instead of >
```

Input: `3 3`, then the roads `1 2`, `2 3`, `3 1` (a triangle).

Wrong output `2 0 0`, correct output `0 0 0`. In the triangle the walk goes $1 \to 2 \to 3$ and the road $3\!-\!1$ gives $\text{low}[2] = \text{low}[3] = 0 = \text{tin}[1]$. A back edge to the parent itself still keeps the part below connected, so for a bridge the test is *strictly* greater (Theorem 4.7.2, step 4); the test with "$\ge$" is the one for articulation points.

**Fix:** use `>`.

### Bug: adding the sizes in the wrong order

Template: towns cut off by a closed road.

```cpp
for (int i = 1; i < n; i++) size[L.parent[L.order[i]]] += size[L.order[i]];   // instead of from n - 1 down to 1
```

Input: `4 3`, then the roads `2 4`, `4 3` and `1 3` (a chain 1–3–4–2).

Wrong output `1 2 2`, correct output `1 2 3`. The visit order is $1, 3, 4, 2$. Going forwards, vertex 3 gives its size (1) to vertex 1 before vertex 4 has given its size to vertex 3, so the sizes of the upper vertices lack their descendants: vertex 3 ends with size 2, not 3. The children must be finished before the parent (Theorem 4.5.2, step 2).

**Fix:** go from the last vertex of the visit order to the first.

### Bug: the first town treated like the others

Template: pieces after a failure.

```cpp
for (int v = 1; v <= n; v++) pieces[v] = 1 + cutOff[v];       // instead of: L.parent[v] ? 1 + cutOff[v] : children[v]
```

Input: `3 2`, then the roads `1 2` and `1 3`.

Wrong output `1 1 1`, correct output `2 1 1`. The walk starts at town 1, which has two children, and when town 1 fails the two towns are separate. For the first town there is no "part above": each child subtree is a piece, and no child has a back edge to something above (Theorem 4.7.3, step 5).

**Fix:** for the first town of a walk, the number of pieces is its number of children.

### Bug: "greater than" in the test for a cut town

Template: pieces after a failure.

```cpp
if (L.parent[p] && L.low[c] > L.tin[p]) cutOff[p]++;          // instead of >=
```

Input: `3 3`, then the roads `2 3`, `2 3` and `1 3`.

Wrong output `1 1 1`, correct output `1 1 2`. The walk goes $1 \to 3 \to 2$, and the second road between 2 and 3 gives $\text{low}[2] = \text{tin}[3] = 1$. A road that goes back to the town itself does not help when this town fails, so the branch below 2 is cut off from town 1 and the town 3 splits the network into two pieces. For an articulation point the test is "$\ge$" (Theorem 4.7.3, step 3).

**Fix:** use `>=`.

### Bug: one position too many in the range of a subtree

Template: roads on every route.

```cpp
L.tin[target] <= L.tin[v] + size[v]                           // instead of < L.tin[v] + size[v]
```

Input: `3 2`, then the roads `2 1` and `1 3`.

Wrong output `2`, correct output `1`. The walk from town 1 meets $1, 2, 3$, so the subtree of town 2 is the single position 1. Town 3 has the position 2, which is $\text{tin}[2] + \text{size}[2]$, the first position after the subtree (Theorem 4.5.3). The road $1\!-\!2$ is a bridge, but town 3 is not behind it.

**Fix:** the subtree of $v$ occupies the positions $\text{tin}[v], \dots, \text{tin}[v] + \text{size}[v] - 1$, so the test is strictly less.

### Bug: the second walk follows the roads, not the reversed roads

Template: kingdoms.

```cpp
for (int v : walk(adj, finish[i], seen)) comp[v] = count;     // instead of walk(rev, finish[i], seen)
```

Input: `7 1`, then the road `6 5`.

Wrong output `6` kingdoms with the labels `1 2 3 4 5 5 6`, correct output `7` kingdoms. In the first walk the towns finish in the order $1, 2, \dots, 7$. The second walk starts at 6 and, following the road $6 \to 5$, takes town 5 into the group of 6. But 5 cannot reach 6. On the reversed roads the walk from 6 finds only the towns that can reach 6 (Theorem 4.7.5, step 5).

**Fix:** walk on the reversed graph.

### Bug: counting the groups with a line leaving

Template: fewest guides.

```cpp
if (comp[v] != comp[to]) entered[comp[v]] = true;             // instead of entered[comp[to]]
```

Input: `3 2`, then the lines `1 2` and `1 3`.

Wrong output `2`, correct output `1`. One guide at stop 1 reaches both other stops. The wrong code marks the group that a line *leaves*, so it counts the groups with no line leaving them (sinks, here 2 and 3). The guides are needed for the groups that no line *enters* (Theorem 4.7.6).

**Fix:** mark `comp[to]`, the group at the end of the line.

## 8. Worked examples

### Example 1: Planets and Kingdoms

[CSES 1683: Planets and Kingdoms](https://cses.fi/problemset/task/1683). In short: a game has $n \le 10^5$ planets and $m \le 2 \cdot 10^5$ one-way teleporters. Two planets belong to the same kingdom exactly when one can travel from each to the other. Print the number of kingdoms and a kingdom label for every planet.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$n = 10^5$, $m = 2 \cdot 10^5$. A search from every planet to find who reaches whom is $n \cdot (n + m) = 3 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps. Two walks of the whole graph are about $6 \cdot 10^5$ steps.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- A search from every planet ([topic 4.1](../4-1-graph-traversal/)) and comparing the reach sets: $3 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps.
- The disjoint set union of [topic 4.4](../4-4-dsu-mst/): it merges both ends of a teleporter, so it ignores the direction and merges planets that are not in one kingdom.
- Groups of planets that reach each other, with one-way edges: the card "Groups that can all reach each other (strongly connected components)".

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The teleporters are one-way and the kingdoms are defined by "a route both from $a$ to $b$ and from $b$ to $a$", which is the definition of a strongly connected component. Decision: two walks, with the reversed graph in the second (Theorem 4.7.5), and print the labels.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

By Theorem 4.7.5, "reach each other" is an equivalence relation, so the planets split into classes, and the classes are the kingdoms of the statement. The second pass of the algorithm collects exactly one class in each walk. The labels $1, \dots, k$ of the walks are therefore a valid answer, and $k$ is the number of kingdoms. The program renames the labels by the first planet in each kingdom; any numbering is accepted.

### Example 2: Flight Routes Check

[CSES 1682: Flight Routes Check](https://cses.fi/problemset/task/1682). In short: $n \le 10^5$ cities and $m \le 2 \cdot 10^5$ one-way flights. Print `YES` if one can travel from every city to every other; otherwise print `NO` and two cities $a$, $b$ such that there is no route from $a$ to $b$.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$n = 10^5$ and $m = 2 \cdot 10^5$. A search from each city is $3 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps; the labels of the groups cost $6 \cdot 10^5$ steps. Only the pair of cities is asked for, no list of routes.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- A search from every city ([topic 4.1](../4-1-graph-traversal/)): $3 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps.
- One search from city 1 on the flights and one on the reversed flights: this decides the question and finds a pair, but the two searches have to be argued separately.
- Reasoning on the groups of cities (the card "Reasoning on the graph of groups (sources and sinks)"): one set of labels gives both the answer and the pair.

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The flights are one-way and the answer depends on whether the graph is one group. Decision: label the groups by Theorem 4.7.5. If there is one group, print `YES`. Otherwise take a city $a$ of the last group and a city $b$ of the first.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

If there is only one group, every city reaches every city by the definition of a group. Otherwise, by Theorem 4.7.6 (a), every flight goes from a city with a smaller or equal group number to a city with a larger or equal number. A route is a chain of flights, so the numbers along it never decrease. Hence a city in the last group cannot reach a city in the first group, and since there are at least two groups, these are different groups: the pair $(a, b)$ is a valid answer.

### Example 3: Critical Connections in a Network

[LeetCode: Critical Connections in a Network](https://leetcode.com/problems/critical-connections-in-a-network/). In short: $n \le 10^5$ servers numbered from 0 are joined by at most $10^5$ two-way connections (no repeated ones) so that every server can reach every other. A connection is critical if its removal makes some server unable to reach another. List all critical connections.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$n, m \le 10^5$. Removing each connection and searching again is $m \cdot (n + m) = 2 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps. One walk is about $3 \cdot 10^5$ steps.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Remove each connection and search ([topic 4.1](../4-1-graph-traversal/)): $2 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps.
- The union structure of [topic 4.4](../4-4-dsu-mst/): it adds connections, it cannot remove them in the middle of a computation.
- A critical connection is a road whose loss disconnects the network: the card "Roads whose loss splits the network (bridges)".

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The connections are two-way and the question is exactly the definition of a bridge. Decision: one walk with entry times and low-link values, and report the tree edges $p\!-\!v$ with $\text{low}[v] > \text{tin}[p]$ (Theorem 4.7.2).

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

The network is connected, so one walk from server 0 covers it, and Theorem 4.7.2 characterises the bridges among the tree edges and says that no other edge is a bridge. A critical connection in the sense of the statement is exactly a bridge: removing it makes some servers unreachable from some others if and only if the number of pieces grows. The walk has to be explicit-stack, since a chain of $10^5$ servers is a valid input. The program prints the connections in the order of the input, and LeetCode accepts any order.
