## 1. Why this topic exists

Here is a small problem. A company has $n = 2 \cdot 10^5$ employees. Employee 1 is the boss, and every other employee has exactly one direct boss. For every employee, print the number of employees below them, directly or through others.

Walking the team of each employee separately is a walk of up to $n$ vertices per employee, up to $n^2 = 4 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps when the company is a long chain. And a recursive walk of a chain this long overflows the stack of a program. A better way uses what a tree is: there is exactly one path from the boss to every employee. One walk from the boss gives every employee a **parent** (their direct boss) and a **depth**, and lists the employees in an order in which every boss comes before everyone below them. Go through this list *backwards*: when an employee is reached, everyone below them has already been counted, so add their count (plus one for themselves) to their boss. One pass, $n$ steps: these counts are the **subtree sizes**.

The same walk answers more questions. If the walk is a depth-first one that writes down the time it enters and leaves each vertex, then every subtree occupies a block of consecutive times (an **Euler tour**), and "is $u$ below $v$?" becomes a comparison of two numbers. The **diameter** (the longest path between two vertices) takes two walks: walk from any vertex to the farthest vertex $a$, and from $a$ to the farthest vertex $b$; the path from $a$ to $b$ is the longest path of the whole tree. Two more walks, from $a$ and from $b$, give for every vertex the distance to its farthest vertex, because that is always one of $a$ and $b$. Each of these costs $O(n)$.

## 3. Definitions

- A **tree** is a connected graph with no cycle. By [Theorem 4.2.2](../4-2-graph-structure/) a connected graph with $n$ vertices is a tree exactly when it has $n - 1$ edges, and in a tree there is exactly one path between any two vertices (Theorem 4.5.1). A tree with weights on the edges has weights as in [topic 4.3](../4-3-shortest-paths/); in this topic they are never negative.
- To **root** a tree means to choose one vertex, the **root**. The **parent** of any other vertex is its neighbour on the path to the root (the same word as in [topic 4.2](../4-2-graph-structure/)), and the other neighbours of a vertex are its **children**. A vertex without children is a **leaf**. The **depth** of a vertex is the number of edges on its path to the root.
- An **ancestor** of $v$ is a vertex on the path from $v$ to the root; in this topic $v$ counts as an ancestor of itself. The **subtree** of $v$ is $v$ and all vertices that have $v$ as an ancestor. The subtree of the root is the whole tree.
- The **distance** between two vertices is the length of the path between them (the sum of its weights; the number of edges when all weights are 1). The **diameter** is the largest distance between two vertices, and the **eccentricity** of a vertex is the largest distance from it to any vertex.
- An **Euler tour** here means the order in which a depth-first walk first reaches the vertices. The position of $v$ in this order is $\text{tin}[v]$ (starting at 0), and $\text{tout}[v]$ is the position of the last vertex of the subtree of $v$.
- A tree with $n = 2 \cdot 10^5$ vertices can be a path of that many vertices, so a recursive walk can go $2 \cdot 10^5$ calls deep; the code here uses a loop with an explicit stack. The code units number the vertices from 1 and use `long long` for sums and distances.


## 4. Theory and proofs

### 4.1 Rooting a tree

**Theorem 4.5.1 (rooting a tree).** Let $T$ be a tree with $n$ vertices and $r$ a vertex. Set $\text{parent}[r] = 0$, $\text{depth}[r] = 0$ and put $r$ on a stack. Repeat: take $u$ from the stack, append it to the list `order`, and for every neighbour $v$ of $u$ other than $\text{parent}[u]$ set $\text{parent}[v] = u$, $\text{depth}[v] = \text{depth}[u] + 1$ and put $v$ on the stack. Then:

(a) between any two vertices of $T$ there is exactly one path;

(b) every vertex is put on the stack exactly once, so `order` has all $n$ vertices; $\text{parent}[v]$ is the neighbour of $v$ on the path from $v$ to $r$, and $\text{depth}[v]$ is the number of edges of this path;

(c) in `order` every vertex comes after its parent;

(d) the time is $O(n)$, and no marks are needed: the only neighbour of $u$ that has been reached before is its parent.

:::tip[In plain words]
In a tree there is only one way to get anywhere. So a walk from the root never meets a vertex twice: the only already-visited neighbour of a vertex is the one it came from. The walk gives each vertex its parent and its depth, and lists every vertex after its parent.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The tree with the edges $1\!-\!2$, $1\!-\!3$, $3\!-\!4$, $3\!-\!5$, rooted at 1. The stack starts as $[1]$. Vertex 1 gives its children 2 and 3. The last one pushed, 3, is taken next and gives 4 and 5; then 5, 4 and 2 follow. The order is $1, 3, 5, 4, 2$, the parents of $1, \dots, 5$ are $0, 1, 1, 3, 3$ and the depths are $0, 1, 1, 2, 2$.

**Proof.**
1. *Claim (a).* The graph is connected, so a path exists. Suppose two different paths from $u$ to $w$ existed. They start at $u$ and, at some vertex $x$, leave through different edges; later they meet again at a first common vertex $y$. The two pieces between $x$ and $y$ form a cycle. A tree has none, so the path is unique.
2. *The walk reaches every vertex, and no marks are needed.* Compare with the walk of Theorem 4.1.1 that marks vertices when it puts them on the container. That walk reaches all vertices, since $T$ is connected, and by Theorem 4.2.2(a) it uses $n - 1$ tree edges. The tree has only $n - 1$ edges, so every edge is a tree edge: there is no non-tree edge, i.e., no edge from a vertex to an already reached vertex other than its parent (Theorem 4.2.2(c)).
3. So when $u$ is taken from the stack, every neighbour of $u$ other than $\text{parent}[u]$ has not been reached yet, and the walk of this theorem (which tests only against the parent) puts exactly the same vertices on the stack as the walk with marks. Each vertex is put on the stack exactly once, from the vertex it is a child of.
4. *Claims (b) and (c).* The vertex $v$ is first reached from $u = \text{parent}[v]$, and by induction on the order of reaching, the chain $v, \text{parent}[v], \text{parent}[\text{parent}[v]], \dots$ is a path to $r$ with $\text{depth}[v]$ edges. By (a) it is the only path. A vertex is put on the stack only while its parent is taken, so it comes later in `order` than its parent.
5. *Claim (d).* Each vertex is put on the stack and taken once, and each edge is looked at twice (from both ends). $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The graph is a tree. With a cycle, a vertex could be reached twice and the walk would need marks (as in topic 4.1); for a tree with a million vertices the marks can be dropped.
2. The vertices are numbered from 1 and `parent[root] = 0` is not a vertex.
3. An explicit stack in place of recursion: a path of $10^6$ vertices would make a recursive walk go $10^6$ calls deep.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.5/thm-1" />

The first example is the tiny instance. The second roots the same tree at vertex 4: the depths are $2, 3, 1, 0, 2$, so the root decides the depths but not the tree. The third has a single vertex. The check relaxes all edges again and again (no walk) on twenty thousand random trees and compares the depths, and checks that every parent comes before its child.

### 4.2 Subtree values

**Theorem 4.5.2 (subtree sums).** Let $T$ be rooted with the `order` and `parent` of Theorem 4.5.1, and let every vertex have a number $a[v]$. Set $\text{sum}[v] = a[v]$ and then go through `order` *backwards*, from the last vertex to the second: add $\text{sum}[v]$ to $\text{sum}[\text{parent}[v]]$. Then at the end $\text{sum}[v]$ is the sum of $a$ over the subtree of $v$, for every $v$. With $a[v] = 1$ for every $v$ this is the size of the subtree, and the time is $O(n)$.

:::tip[In plain words]
Go through the vertices from the last visited to the first. When a vertex is reached, all its descendants have already been added into it, so its total is final; hand the total to the parent. No recursion is needed.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The tree of the previous theorem with the order $1, 3, 5, 4, 2$ and $a = 10, 20, 30, 40, 50$. Backwards: vertex 2 adds 20 to vertex 1 (sum 30). Vertex 4 adds 40 to vertex 3 (sum 70). Vertex 5 adds 50 to vertex 3 (sum 120). Vertex 3 adds 120 to vertex 1 (sum 150). The subtree sums of $1, \dots, 5$ are $150, 20, 120, 40, 50$.

**Proof.**
1. Let $S(v)$ be the subtree of $v$. For $v \ne r$, the children of $v$ are the vertices whose parent is $v$, and $S(v) = \{v\} \cup \bigcup_{c \text{ child of } v} S(c)$, a disjoint union, because the path from the root to a vertex of $S(c)$ goes through $c$ and then $v$ (Theorem 4.5.1(a)).
2. Every child of $v$ comes after $v$ in `order` (Theorem 4.5.1(c)). So in the backwards pass, each child $c$ of $v$ is handled before $v$ is.
3. By induction on the backwards pass: when $v$ is handled, $\text{sum}[v]$ equals $a[v]$ plus the sums of all its children (each child added its final value when it was handled), and by the induction hypothesis these are the sums over $S(c)$; so $\text{sum}[v] = \sum_{u \in S(v)} a[u]$ by step 1. Handing this value to the parent keeps the hypothesis for the parent.
4. Each vertex is handled once with one addition: $O(n)$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Parents come before children in `order` (Theorem 4.5.1(c)), so the backwards pass finishes the children first. Any order with this property works; a breadth-first order also does.
2. The combination of the children's values is a sum (or a count, a maximum, a minimum): the value of $v$ may depend only on $a[v]$ and the values of its children, not on a table that depends on which child.
3. Sums can be large: use `long long`.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.5/thm-2" />

The first two examples are the tiny instance (sizes $5, 1, 3, 1, 1$ with $a = 1$ everywhere, and the sums $150, 20, 120, 40, 50$). The third is a single vertex. The check compares the sums with a count made separately for every vertex by following the parents of all the others.

### 4.3 Subtrees as ranges

**Theorem 4.5.3 (Euler tour).** Let `order` be the list of Theorem 4.5.1 and $\text{tin}[v]$ the position of $v$ in it (from 0). Let $\text{size}[v]$ be the size of the subtree of $v$ and $\text{tout}[v] = \text{tin}[v] + \text{size}[v] - 1$. Then:

(a) the subtree of $v$ consists exactly of the vertices in the positions $\text{tin}[v], \dots, \text{tout}[v]$ of `order`;

(b) $u$ is an ancestor of $v$ (or $u = v$) exactly when $\text{tin}[u] \le \text{tin}[v] \le \text{tout}[u]$;

(c) $\text{tin}$, $\text{tout}$ and the sizes are found in $O(n)$ time.

:::tip[In plain words]
The walk goes deep first: once it enters a vertex, it visits the whole subtree before it takes anything that was waiting. So each subtree is a block of consecutive positions, and "is $u$ above $v$?" is a comparison of numbers.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The tree of Theorem 4.5.1 with the order $1, 3, 5, 4, 2$ gives $\text{tin} = 0, 4, 1, 3, 2$ for $1, \dots, 5$ and the sizes $5, 1, 3, 1, 1$. The ranges are $[0, 4]$ for vertex 1, $[4, 4]$ for 2, $[1, 3]$ for 3 (the vertices 3, 5, 4), $[3, 3]$ for 4 and $[2, 2]$ for 5. Vertex 3 is an ancestor of vertex 4 because $1 \le 3 \le 3$, and not of vertex 2 because $4 > 3$.

**Proof.**
1. When $v$ is taken from the stack, the vertices below it on the stack are those that were waiting before; the children of $v$ are put on top of them. A vertex stays on the stack until everything above it has been taken.
2. So after $v$ is taken, everything that is put on the stack on account of $v$'s descendants is taken before any of the vertices that were below $v$. This means that, starting at the position of $v$, the walk takes the whole subtree of $v$ without interruption, and then continues with the vertices that were below. The subtree is the block of $\text{size}[v]$ consecutive positions starting at $\text{tin}[v]$: this is (a).
3. For (b): if $u$ is an ancestor of $v$, then $v \in S(u)$, which by (a) means $\text{tin}[u] \le \text{tin}[v] \le \text{tout}[u]$. Conversely, if $\text{tin}[v]$ is in this range, then $v$ is in the block of $S(u)$, so it is in $S(u)$, and $u$ is an ancestor of $v$.
4. Claim (c): the sizes come from Theorem 4.5.2 and the positions from one pass over `order`. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The walk takes the most recently pushed vertex first (a stack). With a queue (a breadth-first order) the subtrees are not blocks.
2. Positions start at 0 in the code; a program that uses 1-based arrays shifts all ranges by one.
3. The ranges answer questions about subtrees with the tools for ranges of an array: sums by prefix sums (topic 1.2), counts of values by sorting and binary search (topic 1.4).
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.5/thm-3" />

The first example prints the ranges of the tiny instance and two ancestor tests. The second is a single vertex. The check compares the ancestor test with climbing the parents for every pair of vertices of twenty thousand random trees.

### 4.4 The diameter

**Theorem 4.5.4 (diameter).** Let $T$ be a tree with non-negative edge weights. Let $x$ be any vertex, $a$ a vertex farthest from $x$, and $b$ a vertex farthest from $a$. Then $d(a, b)$ is the diameter of $T$. The time is $O(n)$: two walks.

:::tip[In plain words]
Start anywhere and go to the vertex farthest from there; it is always one end of some longest path. From it, go to the farthest vertex; that gives the longest path. Two walks are enough.
:::

**A tiny instance.** Edges $1\!-\!2$ (3), $2\!-\!3$ (4), $2\!-\!4$ (1), $4\!-\!5$ (6), $1\!-\!6$ (2). From vertex 1 the distances are $0, 3, 7, 4, 10, 2$, so the farthest vertex is $a = 5$ (distance 10). From 5 the distances are $10$ to vertex 1, $7$ to 2, $11$ to 3, $6$ to 4 and $12$ to 6, so $b = 6$, and the diameter is $12$, on the path $5, 4, 2, 1, 6$.

**Proof.**
1. Let $(u, v)$ be a diameter, with $d(u, v) = D$, and $P$ the path between them. In a tree, for two vertices $p$ and $q$, $d(p, q)$ is the length of the only path, and for any three vertices $d(p, q) \le d(p, r) + d(r, q)$ (this uses weights $\ge 0$: a detour never gets shorter).
2. Let $m$ be the vertex of $P$ closest to $x$, and $t$ the vertex of $P$ closest to $a$. Every path from $x$ to a vertex of $P$ passes through $m$, and every path from $a$ to a vertex of $P$ passes through $t$. By symmetry we may assume that $t$ is on the $v$ side of $m$ (or $t = m$), meaning $d(m, v) = d(m, t) + d(t, v)$.
3. Since $a$ is farthest from $x$, $d(x, a) \ge d(x, v) = d(x, m) + d(m, t) + d(t, v)$.
4. On the other hand $d(x, a) \le d(x, m) + d(m, t) + d(t, a)$ by the triangle inequality.
5. From steps 3 and 4: $d(t, a) \ge d(t, v)$.
6. The path from $a$ to $u$ passes through $t$ (as $t$ is the vertex of $P$ closest to $a$), so $d(a, u) = d(a, t) + d(t, u) \ge d(t, v) + d(t, u) = D$. So $a$ is an end of a path of length $D$, and the farthest vertex $b$ from $a$ satisfies $d(a, b) \ge D$. It cannot be more than $D$, the diameter; so $d(a, b) = D$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Weights are not negative (step 1). With a negative edge, a detour can be shorter and the farthest vertex need not be an end of a longest path.
2. The graph is a tree: the paths in steps 2 and 6 are the only ones (Theorem 4.5.1(a)).
3. Both walks are walks of a tree from a given vertex, with the weights added (the walk of Theorem 4.5.1 with distances).
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.5/thm-4" />

The first example is the tiny instance (12 between 5 and 6). The second is a single vertex, with diameter 0. The third has all weights 0, where every vertex is farthest and the diameter is 0. The check compares the result with the largest entry of a table of all distances (Floyd–Warshall, topic 4.3) on twenty thousand random trees with weights 0 to 1 or 0 to 9.

### 4.5 The farthest vertex from every vertex

**Theorem 4.5.5 (farthest vertices).** Let $T$ be a tree with non-negative weights, and let $a$ and $b$ be the ends of a diameter found as in Theorem 4.5.4. Then for every vertex $v$, the largest distance from $v$ to any vertex is $\max(d(v, a), d(v, b))$. All these values come from two more walks, from $a$ and from $b$.

:::tip[In plain words]
Whatever vertex you stand on, the vertex farthest from you is one of the two ends of the longest path. So walk from each end once, and take the larger of the two distances at every vertex.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The star with centre 1 and leaves 2, 3, 4 at the distances 5, 2 and 3. The diameter is from leaf 2 to leaf 4: $5 + 3 = 8$. For vertex 1 the larger distance to the ends is $\max(5, 3) = 5$; for 2, $\max(0, 8) = 8$; for 3, $\max(7, 5) = 7$; for 4, $\max(8, 0) = 8$.

**Proof.**
1. Let $a$, $b$ be the ends of a diameter with $D = d(a, b)$ and $P$ the path between them. Let $v$ be any vertex and $z$ any vertex. We show $d(v, z) \le \max(d(v, a), d(v, b))$; the maximum is attained at $a$ or $b$ itself, so this proves the claim.
2. Let $m$ be the vertex of $P$ closest to $v$ and $t$ the vertex of $P$ closest to $z$. By symmetry assume $t$ is on the $b$ side of $m$ (or $t = m$).
3. Then $d(v, b) = d(v, m) + d(m, t) + d(t, b)$.
4. By the triangle inequality, $d(v, z) \le d(v, m) + d(m, t) + d(t, z)$.
5. We have $d(t, z) \le d(t, b)$: otherwise the path from $z$ to $a$ through $t$ would have length $d(z, t) + d(t, a) > d(t, b) + d(t, a) = D$, longer than the diameter.
6. From steps 4 and 5: $d(v, z) \le d(v, m) + d(m, t) + d(t, b) = d(v, b)$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. $a$ and $b$ are the ends of a diameter, which Theorem 4.5.4 guarantees for the pair found by the two walks.
2. Weights are not negative, as in the previous theorem.
3. For a vertex $v$, the vertex farthest from it is not unique in general; the value $\max(d(v, a), d(v, b))$ is.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.5/thm-5" />

The first example is a path of four vertices (farthest distances $3, 2, 2, 3$). The second is the star of the tiny instance ($5, 8, 7, 8$). The third is a single vertex. The check compares the values with the largest entry of each row of Floyd–Warshall's table for twenty thousand random trees.

### 4.6 The sum of all distances

**Theorem 4.5.6 (sum over all pairs).** Let $T$ be a rooted tree with non-negative weights, and for every non-root vertex $v$ let $w_v$ be the weight of the edge to its parent and $s_v$ the size of its subtree. Then the sum of $d(u, v)$ over all pairs of vertices $u < v$ is
$$\sum_{v \ne r} w_v \cdot s_v \cdot (n - s_v).$$
The time is $O(n)$.

:::tip[In plain words]
Cut an edge: the tree falls into two parts, and a path between two vertices uses the edge exactly when its ends are in different parts. So an edge is used by (size of one part) times (size of the other part) pairs, and pays its weight each time.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The path $1 - 2 - 3$ with both weights 1, rooted at 1. Vertex 2 has $s = 2$: $1 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 2$. Vertex 3 has $s = 1$: $1 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 2$. The total is 4, and the pairs agree: $d(1, 2) + d(2, 3) + d(1, 3) = 1 + 1 + 2 = 4$.

**Proof.**
1. For a non-root vertex $v$, removing the edge between $v$ and its parent splits $T$ into the subtree of $v$ ($s_v$ vertices) and the rest ($n - s_v$ vertices).
2. A path between $u$ and $w$ is the unique path (Theorem 4.5.1(a)); it uses the edge exactly when $u$ and $w$ are in different parts, since the edge is the only link between the parts.
3. The number of pairs with one vertex in each part is $s_v (n - s_v)$.
4. The distance $d(u, w)$ is the sum of the weights of the edges on the path, so summing over all pairs, each edge contributes its weight once for each pair that uses it: $\sum_v w_v s_v (n - s_v)$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Unique paths: in a graph with cycles, the number of pairs that use an edge is not a product.
2. The sizes $s_v$ come from Theorem 4.5.2.
3. The result can be as large as about $n^3 w / 2$, which needs `long long` already for $n = 10^5$ with small weights.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="4.5/thm-6" />

The first two examples are the path (4) and the star with weights 5, 2, 3 (30). The third is a single vertex. The check compares the result with the sum of the upper triangle of Floyd–Warshall's table for twenty thousand random trees.

## 6. Templates

Seven templates. Each shows only the function that matters; the program around it reads the input. The functions use the structure `Rooted` and the function `rootAt` of Theorem 4.5.1 (the walk with an explicit stack) and the function `farthestFrom` of Theorem 4.5.4 (distances from one vertex of a weighted tree, returning a farthest vertex), where they are named. Vertices are numbered from 1, and `adj` has `n + 1` entries. Each template is checked against a brute force with a different method (relaxing distances again and again, Floyd–Warshall, cutting each edge and counting, climbing the parents) on thousands of random small trees, and on fixed tests with a single vertex, a path, a star, zero weights and weights near $10^9$.

### Vertices at each depth (Theorem 4.5.1)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.5/level-counts" />

**How it works.** `rootAt(adj, 1)` gives `depth[v]` for every vertex. The deepest vertex tells how many levels there are, and one pass over the vertices counts them per level. The walk uses a stack in a loop, so a tree that is a path of $2 \cdot 10^5$ vertices is no problem.

**Alternative formulation.** A queue in place of the stack makes the walk go level by level, and the counts can be taken from the queue as it runs. If the tree is given by a parent array, the depths are computed in the order of the numbers when parents have smaller numbers: `depth[i] = depth[parent[i]] + 1`.

### Sums over the subtrees, parents with smaller numbers (Theorem 4.5.2)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.5/subtree-sums" />

**How it works.** When the input says "the parent of vertex $i$ is $p_i$ with $p_i < i$", the numbers $1, \dots, n$ already are an order in which every vertex comes after its parent, so no walk is needed. The loop goes from $n$ down to 2: when vertex $i$ is reached, all its children (which have larger numbers) have been added into it already, so `sum[i]` is final and it is added into its parent. The array `sum` is a copy of the input values, so the function can also use sizes (all ones) or counts.

**Alternative formulation.** When the parents are not smaller, build `adj`, walk with `rootAt` and use `t.order` backwards, as in the theorem. Replacing `+=` by `max` computes the largest value in each subtree.

### How often is each road used? (Theorem 4.5.6)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.5/edge-usage" />

**How it works.** The tree is rooted at 1 and the sizes of the subtrees are computed backwards over the visit order. For a road $(a, b)$, one of the ends is the parent of the other; the endpoint that is the child is `a` if `parent[a] == b`, otherwise `b`. Removing the road splits the tree into the subtree of the child, with `size[child]` vertices, and the rest. A trip between two towns uses the road exactly when the towns are on different sides, and there are `size * (n - size)` such pairs, in 64 bits.

**Alternative formulation.** To get the sum of all distances, multiply each count by the length of its road and add (the formula of Theorem 4.5.6).

### Is $u$ an ancestor of $v$? (Theorem 4.5.3)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.5/ancestor-queries" />

**How it works.** The walk gives the visit order, `tin[v]` is the position of $v$ in it, and `size[v]` comes from the backward pass. The subtree of $u$ occupies the positions `tin[u]` to `tin[u] + size[u] - 1`, so $u$ is an ancestor of $v$ (or $v = u$) exactly when `tin[v]` lies in this block. Each question takes a comparison of three numbers.

**Alternative formulation.** For questions about the vertices of the subtree (their sum, how many have a property), keep the values in an array in the order of the visit and take prefix sums; the answer for $u$ is the sum over the block. For counts of a value in a block, keep the sorted positions per value and use two binary searches.

### The longest road trip (Theorem 4.5.4)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.5/diameter" />

**How it works.** `farthestFrom(adj, s, dist)` fills `dist` with the distances from `s` and returns a vertex at the largest distance. The first call, from town 1, returns a town `a`; the second call, from `a`, returns the farthest town `b`, and `dist[b]` is the diameter. All lengths are added in 64 bits; zero lengths are allowed.

**Alternative formulation.** The two ends `a` and `b` are the ends of a longest path; to print the path itself, store the parent of each town in the second walk and follow it from `b` to `a`. For "the shortest trip that visits every town and need not return", print $2 \cdot (\text{sum of all lengths}) - \text{diameter}$.

### The farthest town from each town (Theorem 4.5.5)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.5/farthest-all" />

**How it works.** The first walk finds a diameter end `a`, the second, from `a`, finds the other end `b` and gives the distances `fromA`; the third gives the distances `fromB` from `b`. For every town the farthest distance is the larger of the two. Three walks, $O(n)$.

**Alternative formulation.** Four walks do the same if the first one is replaced by a call from any town to find `a`, as written. If the distances to the ends are needed again, keep `fromA` and `fromB`: the farthest town of `v` is `a` when `fromA[v] >= fromB[v]` and `b` otherwise.

### Where to meet (Theorems 4.5.4 and 4.5.5)

<CodeSnippet unit="4.5/meeting-place" />

**How it works.** With `fromA` and `fromB` from the previous template, the value for town $v$ is `max(fromA[v], fromB[v])`, and the function looks for the town with the smallest value. The comparison is strict, so a later town replaces the best one only if it is strictly better, and the smallest number wins a tie. The best towns lie on the middle of the longest path.

**Alternative formulation.** The smallest possible largest distance is half the diameter when the tree allows a split in the middle of a road (a tree with only whole towns allowed rounds up). For "the smallest height of the tree over all choices of the root", print this value with all weights 1.

## 7. Bug catalogue

Each bug below was made by changing a template from Section 6 and caught by `scripts/stress.sh`. The inputs shown are small failing inputs, checked against the brute force.

### Bug: walking back into the parent

Template: vertices at each depth (the walk of Theorem 4.5.1).

```cpp
if (true) {                            // instead of if (v != t.parent[u])
```

Input: `2`, then the road `1 2`.

The program never ends (it is stopped by the time limit). From vertex 1 the walk goes to 2; from 2 it sees the neighbour 1 again, goes back, and so on. A tree needs no marks only because the parent is skipped (Theorem 4.5.1, step 3).

**Fix:** skip the neighbour that is the parent of the vertex.

### Bug: adding up in the order of the numbers

Template: sums over the subtrees, parents with smaller numbers.

```cpp
for (int i = 2; i <= n; i++) sum[parent[i]] += sum[i];     // instead of from n down to 2
```

Input: `3`, then the numbers `8 5 7` and the parents `1 2` (vertex 2 under 1, vertex 3 under 2).

Wrong output `13 12 7`, correct output `20 12 7`. Going forwards, vertex 2 hands its sum (5) to vertex 1 *before* vertex 3 has handed its 7 to vertex 2, so the 7 never reaches vertex 1. The children must be finished before the parent (Theorem 4.5.2, step 2).

**Fix:** go from the largest number to the smallest.

### Bug: multiplying the size by itself

Template: how often is each road used?

```cpp
used.push_back(size[child] * size[child]);       // instead of size[child] * (n - size[child])
```

Input: `3`, then the roads `3 1` and `2 1`.

Wrong output `1 1`, correct output `2 2`. Each road leads to a leaf and splits the three towns into 1 and 2; the pairs that use it are those with one town on each side: $1 \cdot 2 = 2$ (Theorem 4.5.6, step 3).

**Fix:** multiply the two sides: `s * (n - s)`.

### Bug: the child of the road is always the first end

Template: how often is each road used?

```cpp
int child = a;                                    // instead of t.parent[a] == b ? a : b
```

Input: `2`, then the road `1 2`.

Wrong output `0`, correct output `1`. The first end is the root here, whose subtree is the whole tree: $2 \cdot (2 - 2) = 0$. The end that is farther from the root is the child, and the roads can be given in either order.

**Fix:** take the end whose parent is the other end.

### Bug: one position too many in the range

Template: is $u$ an ancestor of $v$?

```cpp
answer.push_back(tin[u] <= tin[v] && tin[v] <= tin[u] + size[u]);   // instead of < tin[u] + size[u]
```

Input: `3`, the parents `1 1`, then one question `3 2`.

Wrong output `YES`, correct output `NO`. The visit order is $1, 3, 2$, so vertex 3 has the position 1 and the size 1: its block is the position 1 only. Vertex 2 has the position 2, which is $\text{tin}[3] + \text{size}[3]$, the first position after the block (Theorem 4.5.3: the block ends at $\text{tin} + \text{size} - 1$).

**Fix:** the block is `tin[u]` to `tin[u] + size[u] - 1`, so the test is strictly less than `tin[u] + size[u]`.

### Bug: one walk for the diameter

Template: the longest road trip.

```cpp
int b = a;                                       // instead of the second walk from a
```

Input: `3`, then the roads `3 1 1` and `2 1 3`.

Wrong output `3`, correct output `4`. The farthest town from town 1 is town 2 (distance 3), but the longest trip is from town 2 to town 3: $3 + 1 = 4$. The first walk finds an end of a longest path, not the path (Theorem 4.5.4).

**Fix:** walk again from the town found and take the farthest distance.

### Bug: only one end of the diameter

Template: the farthest town from each town.

```cpp
best[v] = fromA[v];                              // instead of max(fromA[v], fromB[v])
```

Input: `2`, then the road `1 2 4`.

Wrong output `4 0`, correct output `4 4`. The walk from the end `a = 2` gives the distance 0 for town 2 itself, but the farthest town from 2 is town 1 at distance 4, which is the other end. Each town needs both ends (Theorem 4.5.5).

**Fix:** take the maximum of the distances to the two ends.

### Bug: the larger number wins a tie

Template: where to meet.

```cpp
if (max(fromA[v], fromB[v]) <= best.second)       // instead of <
```

Input: `2`, then the road `1 2 4`.

Wrong output `2 4`, correct output `1 4`. Both towns have the farthest distance 4, and the task asks for the smallest number; with `<=` the later town replaces the earlier one.

**Fix:** replace the best only when strictly better.

## 8. Worked examples

### Example 1: Erase Leaves

[AtCoder Beginner Contest (ABC) 333 D: Erase Leaves](https://atcoder.jp/contests/abc333/tasks/abc333_d). In short: a tree has $N \le 3 \cdot 10^5$ vertices. One operation deletes a leaf (a vertex of degree at most 1) together with its edge. Find the fewest operations needed to delete vertex 1.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$N = 3 \cdot 10^5$. Trying all sequences of deletions is hopeless. One walk and one backward pass are about $10^6$ steps.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Try every sequence of leaf deletions ([topic 0.5](../../phase-0/0-5-brute-force/)): exponential.
- The tree rooted at vertex 1 falls into the subtrees under vertex 1, and the number of vertices of each decides the cost: the card "Subtree sizes, sums and counts".

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

Vertex 1 becomes a leaf when at most one of its neighbours is left. The vertices of a subtree under vertex 1 can be deleted only from the bottom up, one by one, so the whole subtree costs its size in operations. Decision: root at 1, compute the sizes of the subtrees, and keep the largest subtree under vertex 1.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

Every vertex other than vertex 1 must be deleted if it lies in a subtree under vertex 1 that is deleted completely, and at least all subtrees under vertex 1 but one must be deleted completely: vertex 1 has degree at most 1 only when at most one neighbour remains, and a subtree under vertex 1 that is not deleted completely still contains its top vertex, the neighbour. Each vertex of a deleted subtree costs one operation. So at least $(n - 1) - s_{\max}$ vertices are deleted before vertex 1, and vertex 1 itself costs one more, giving at least $n - s_{\max}$ operations, where $s_{\max}$ is the size of the largest subtree under vertex 1 (the remaining subtree can be any one, and the largest keeps the most). The bound is reached: delete every other subtree from the bottom up (leaves of the whole tree first, one at a time), then vertex 1 is a leaf. The sizes come from Theorem 4.5.2.

### Example 2: Tree and Hamilton Path 2

[AtCoder Beginner Contest (ABC) 361 E: Tree and Hamilton Path 2](https://atcoder.jp/contests/abc361/tasks/abc361_e). In short: $N \le 2 \cdot 10^5$ cities are joined by $N - 1$ roads with lengths up to $10^9$ so that every city can be reached from every other. Find the least total distance of a trip that visits every city at least once; it may start anywhere and need not return.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$N = 2 \cdot 10^5$; trying all orders of the cities is impossible. The answer can be about $2 \cdot 10^{14}$, so 64-bit numbers are needed. Two walks are $4 \cdot 10^5$ steps.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Try all orders of visiting the cities ([topic 0.5](../../phase-0/0-5-brute-force/)): $N!$ orders.
- A tree, a trip that covers all roads, and a saving that depends on the longest path: the card "Longest path (diameter)".

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The roads form a tree (connected, $N - 1$ roads) with positive lengths. Decision: the answer is twice the total length minus the diameter, with the diameter found by two walks.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

To visit every city, every road must be driven at least once. A trip that visits all cities and returns to the start crosses each road exactly twice in the cheapest case (every road must be left and entered again). Such a closed trip has the total $2L$, where $L$ is the sum of all lengths. A trip that does not return starts at $s$ and ends at $t$; compared with the closed trip, the roads on the path from $s$ to $t$ are driven once instead of twice, so it saves exactly the length of this path and costs $2L - d(s, t)$. The best choice makes $d(s, t)$ as large as possible, which is the diameter (Theorem 4.5.4). A trip cannot do better, since a road not on the path from $s$ to $t$ must be driven twice (once to enter the part behind it and once to leave it), and a road on the path at least once.

### Example 3: Count Descendants

[AtCoder Beginner Contest (ABC) 202 E: Count Descendants](https://atcoder.jp/contests/abc202/tasks/abc202_e). In short: a rooted tree has $N \le 2 \cdot 10^5$ vertices (root 1, the parent of vertex $i$ is $P_i < i$), and there are $Q \le 2 \cdot 10^5$ questions $(U, D)$: how many vertices in the subtree of $U$ have depth exactly $D$?

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$N, Q = 2 \cdot 10^5$. A walk of the subtree for each question is up to $4 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps. A sort-free preparation plus two binary searches per question is $2 \cdot 10^5 \cdot 2 \cdot 18 \approx 7 \cdot 10^6$.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- A walk of the subtree of $U$ for every question ([topic 4.1](../4-1-graph-traversal/)): too slow.
- Many questions about the content of subtrees on a fixed rooted tree: the card "Subtrees as ranges (Euler tour)".

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The root is fixed (vertex 1) and every question is about a subtree plus one more property, the depth. Decision: number the vertices in the order of a walk, so that each subtree is a block of positions; for every depth, store the sorted list of positions of its vertices; count the positions of depth $D$ inside the block of $U$ by two binary searches ([topic 1.4](../../phase-1/1-4-binary-search/)).

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

By Theorem 4.5.3(a), the vertices of the subtree of $U$ are exactly those with positions from $\text{tin}[U]$ to $\text{tin}[U] + \text{size}[U] - 1$. The vertices at depth $D$ are exactly the vertices whose positions are in the list of depth $D$. A vertex is counted when it is in both sets, so the answer is the number of positions of the list that lie in the block. The list is sorted because it is filled while going through the positions in increasing order, so `lower_bound` and `upper_bound` count the positions in the block in $O(\log N)$ each. A depth that does not occur has an empty list, and a depth of $N$ or more is answered with 0 directly.
