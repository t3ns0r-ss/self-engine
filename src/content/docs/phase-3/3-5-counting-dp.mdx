## 1. Why this topic exists

Here is a small problem. A robot walks on an $n \times n$ grid with $n = 1000$, from the top-left cell to the bottom-right cell, moving only right or down. Some cells are blocked. How many different walks are there? Print the number modulo $10^9 + 7$.

Without blocked cells the answer is a binomial coefficient ([topic 2.3](../../phase-2/2-3-combinatorics/)): a walk is a choice of which $n - 1$ of its $2n - 2$ moves go down, $\binom{1998}{999}$, a number with 600 digits. With blocked cells the formula breaks, and listing the walks one by one ([topic 3.1](../3-1-backtracking/)) is hopeless.

The key idea: every walk that reaches a cell arrives either from the cell above or from the cell to the left, and the two groups do not overlap. So the number of walks to a cell is the sum of two numbers already computed, $10^6$ additions in all, each taken modulo the prime ([topic 2.2](../../phase-2/2-2-modular-arithmetic/)). This is the counting form of the tables of [topic 3.2](../3-2-dp-foundations/). The same idea counts strings that obey local rules, sums of allowed parts, and tables whose transitions add up a whole range of earlier entries, which prefix sums make fast.

## 3. Definitions

- A **monotone path** in a grid moves only right or down (or, in other problems, only in a fixed set of directions that never lead back). Example: in a $2 \times 3$ grid there are 3 monotone paths from the top-left to the bottom-right cell.
- **Counting modulo $p$** means computing the number of objects, which may be astronomically large, as its remainder modulo a prime $p$ such as $10^9 + 7$ or $998244353$.
- An **acyclic order** on states is a numbering of the states such that every transition goes from a smaller number to a larger one. Example: in a grid with right and down moves, reading the cells row by row, left to right, is such an order.
- A **composition** of $n$ is an ordered sum of positive parts equal to $n$; an **integer partition** of $n$ is an unordered one. Example: $3 = 1 + 2 = 2 + 1 = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$ gives 4 compositions but 3 partitions.
- A **range transition** is a rule $\text{dp}[i][j] = \sum_{k = l}^{r} \text{dp}[i-1][k]$, where the earlier entries summed form a contiguous range of indices.

## 4. Theory and proofs

### 4.1 The sum rule

Theorem 3.2.2 already counts objects by their last choice. This section states the rule in the form used for paths and strings, with the two conditions that are easy to break, and adds the remainders.

**Theorem 3.5.1 (counting by the last step).** Suppose every object to be counted (a path, a sequence) ends in a state, and removing its last step gives an object that ends in an earlier state, with the following properties: (a) removing the last step is one-to-one on the objects that end in state $s$ and come from state $t$; (b) each object ending in $s$ comes from exactly one state $t$. Then
$$\text{cnt}[s] = \sum_{t \to s} \text{cnt}[t],$$
summed over the states $t$ with a step to $s$. Filling the states in an acyclic order gives every count. All of this can be done modulo $p$: reduce after each addition.

*Proof.* Split the objects ending in $s$ by the state $t$ they come from. By (b) the groups do not overlap and cover everything. By (a), removing the last step maps the group of $t$ one-to-one onto the objects ending in $t$ (every object ending in $t$ can be extended by the step $t \to s$, and two different objects stay different after the same step is added). So the group of $t$ has $\text{cnt}[t]$ objects. In an acyclic order every $t$ comes before $s$, so its count is known when $s$ is filled. Remainders modulo $p$ can be added and reduced at every step without changing the final remainder (topic 2.2). $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Every object comes from exactly **one** earlier state. If two different steps could lead to the same object, it is counted twice; if the state does not determine what may come next, objects can be missed.
2. The steps never lead back, so an acyclic order exists (for a grid with right and down moves: row by row).
3. For a minimum or a maximum (cheapest path) the same table works with min or max in place of the sum (Theorem 3.2.1).
:::

Grid with right and down moves, `#` blocked; each cell holds the number of paths from the top-left cell:

| | col 1 | col 2 | col 3 | col 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| row 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| row 2 | 1 | `#` (0) | 1 | 2 |
| row 3 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 4 |

The answer is 4. Check with topic 2.3: without the block there are $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ paths; the paths through the blocked cell are $2 \cdot 3 = 6$; $10 - 6 = 4$.

### 4.2 Sequences with a small state

**Theorem 3.5.2 (counting with a state).** Count the sequences of length $n$ over an alphabet, where whether a letter may be added depends only on a **state** of the sequence so far, and the new state is a function $\delta(s, x)$ of the old state $s$ and the letter $x$. Let $\text{cnt}[i][s]$ be the number of valid sequences of length $i$ whose state is $s$. Then
$$\text{cnt}[i][s'] = \sum_{(s, x):\ x \text{ allowed in } s,\ \delta(s, x) = s'} \text{cnt}[i-1][s],$$
and the answer is the sum of $\text{cnt}[n][s]$ over all states. Time $O(n \cdot (\text{states}) \cdot (\text{letters}))$.

*Proof.* This is Theorem 3.5.1 with the pairs (length, state) as the states, as in Theorem 3.2.5. A valid sequence of length $i$ has exactly one last letter $x$ and exactly one state $s$ before it, because the state is a function of the sequence; removing $x$ gives a valid sequence of length $i - 1$ with state $s$. Two different sequences with the same $(s, x)$ differ before the last letter, so removing it is one-to-one. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The state is **determined** by the sequence so far (a function, not a guess), so each sequence is counted once; and it holds everything the rules look at.
2. The number of states is small: the last letter, the length of the current run, a remainder, a few flags.
:::

Example: strings over $\{a, b, c\}$ with no "ab" inside. The rule only looks at the previous letter, and only at whether it is $a$, so two states are enough: "ends with $a$" and "ends with something else". From "ends with $a$", the letters $a$ (state $a$) and $c$ (other) are allowed; from "other", all three are. Starting with 1 and 2 for length 1:

| length | ends with $a$ | other | total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| 2 | 3 | 5 | 8 |
| 3 | 8 | 13 | 21 |
| 4 | 21 | 34 | 55 |

### 4.3 Sums of parts, and "at least one"

Counting sums of allowed parts is the counting knapsack of topic 3.3: for compositions (ordered) the totals are the outer loop, $f[m] = \sum_{q} f[m - q]$ over the allowed parts $q \le m$; for partitions (unordered) the parts are the outer loop (Theorem 3.3.3). Many problems add a condition such as "at least one part is at least $d$" or "the last part is even". Two tools handle them: add a flag to the state (Theorem 3.5.2), or count the complement.

**Theorem 3.5.3 (complement modulo a prime).** If $A$ is the number of all objects and $B$ the number of objects that violate the condition, then the number that satisfy it is $A - B$; modulo $p$ it is $(A \bmod p - B \bmod p + p) \bmod p$.

*Proof.* The objects that satisfy the condition and those that violate it split all objects into two disjoint groups, so their counts add up to $A$. The remainder of a difference is the difference of the remainders, adjusted into $0..p-1$ by adding $p$ once, since both remainders lie in $0..p-1$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. Both counts are computed exactly modulo the same $p$, and "satisfies" is exactly "does not violate".
2. In C++, `(A - B) % p` can be negative when $A < B$ as remainders; add $p$ before the final `% p`.
:::

Example: compositions of 4 with parts from $\{1, 2, 3\}$: $f = 1, 1, 2, 4, 7$ for $m = 0..4$ (1111, 112, 121, 211, 22, 13, 31). Those with all parts below 2 use only the part 1: one composition. So 6 compositions of 4 use at least one part $\ge 2$.

### 4.4 Range transitions with prefix sums

**Theorem 3.5.4 (prefix sums of a row).** Suppose $\text{dp}[i][j] = \sum_{k = l(j)}^{r(j)} \text{dp}[i-1][k]$ for ranges $[l(j), r(j)]$. Let $P[k] = \text{dp}[i-1][0] + \dots + \text{dp}[i-1][k-1]$, with $P[0] = 0$. Then $\text{dp}[i][j] = P[r(j) + 1] - P[l(j)]$, so each row costs $O(\text{row length})$ instead of $O(\text{row length}^2)$. Modulo $p$, compute $P$ modulo $p$ and subtract as in Theorem 3.5.3.

*Proof.* $P[r + 1] - P[l]$ is the sum of the entries with index $l$ to $r$ (topic 1.2); computing $P$ takes one pass over the row. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The entries summed form a **contiguous** range of one row that is already complete (it belongs to an earlier step).
2. The range ends are clipped to the row: $l \ge 0$ and $r \le$ the last index.
3. If the range lies in the same row being filled (for example $\text{dp}[j] = \sum_{k = j - d}^{j - 1} \text{dp}[k]$), keep the prefix sums growing as entries are filled; each entry is used only after it is final.
:::

Example: give $K = 3$ identical candies to 3 children, who can take at most 2, 1 and 2 candies. Let $\text{dp}[i][j]$ be the ways for the first $i$ children to take $j$ candies; child $i$ with cap $c$ takes $t = 0..c$ candies, so $\text{dp}[i][j] = \sum_{k = j - c}^{j} \text{dp}[i-1][k]$, a range:

| $j$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| no child | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| child 1 (cap 2) | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| child 2 (cap 1) | 1 | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| child 3 (cap 2) | 1 | 3 | 5 | 5 |

The answer is 5: (2, 1, 0), (2, 0, 1), (1, 1, 1), (1, 0, 2), (0, 1, 2).

### 4.5 Probabilities

When every step is chosen uniformly at random (a knight picks one of its 8 moves), the probability of an outcome after $k$ steps is the number of step sequences that produce it divided by the total number of sequences. So a probability table is the counting table with each transition multiplied by the probability of the step, here $1/8$, computed with doubles or as a fraction modulo $p$ (topic 2.2). Theorems 3.5.1 and 3.5.2 hold with these weights: the proof splits the outcomes by the last step in the same way.

## 6. Templates

### Paths in a grid (Theorem 3.5.1)

<Code code={gridPaths} lang="cpp" title="grid-paths/solution.cpp" />

**How it works.** One row of counts is kept. When cell $(r, c)$ is reached, `ways[c]` still holds the count of the cell above (from the previous row), and `ways[c - 1]` already holds the count of the cell to the left (this row). Their sum is the count for $(r, c)$, by Theorem 3.5.1. A blocked cell gets 0, so no path continues from it. The cells are filled in reading order, which is an acyclic order for right and down moves.

**C++ details explained.** `1'000'000'007` uses digit separators (C++14), which only make the number easier to read. The counts are `long long`: the sum of two remainders below $10^9 + 7$ is about $2 \cdot 10^9$, more than an `int` holds. `for (auto& row : g) cin >> row;` reads each line into the vector by reference.

**Tested.** Stress-tested against a brute force that walks every right/down path by recursion, on 5000 random grids (up to $8 \times 8$, with 0–40% blocked cells, including blocked start or end cells). There are also 5 fixed tests: the grid of Section 4.1 (4), a single cell (1), a blocked start (0), an empty $3000 \times 3000$ grid (checked against $\binom{5998}{2999}$ modulo $10^9 + 7$), and a random $3000 \times 3000$ grid whose answer comes from a separate program.

**Alternative formulation.** Keep the full table `ways[r][c]` with a border row and column of zeros, and set `ways[1][1] = 1`; then every cell is `ways[r-1][c] + ways[r][c-1]` without special cases. It uses $H \cdot W$ memory but is easier to reconstruct a path from.

### Strings with limited runs (Theorem 3.5.2)

<Code code={runLimit} lang="cpp" title="run-limit/solution.cpp" />

**How it works.** The rule "no letter more than $r$ times in a row" only looks at the length of the last run, so that is the state. A new letter either differs from the last one ($k - 1$ choices, the run length becomes 1) or repeats it (one choice, the run grows by 1, allowed only below $r$). The state after adding a letter is determined by the old state and the letter, so each string is counted once (Theorem 3.5.2). Time $O(n \cdot r)$.

**C++ details explained.** `vector<long long> next(r + 1, 0)` builds the new row from the old one, so no entry is read after it has been overwritten; `cnt = next` then copies it over. `total * (k - 1) % MOD` stays below $9.2 \cdot 10^{18}$ because both factors are below $10^9 + 7$ and $26$.

**Tested.** Stress-tested against a brute force that lists all $k^n$ strings as numbers in base $k$, on 5000 random inputs ($k \le 4$, $n$ up to 14, $r \le 5$). There are also 5 fixed tests: $n = 4$, $k = 3$, $r = 1$ ($3 \cdot 2^3 = 24$), one letter of length 1 (1), $n = 10^5$ with one letter (0), and two inputs with $n = 10^5$ whose answers come from a separate program.

**Alternative formulation.** Count by runs instead of letters: a string is a sequence of runs with lengths in $1..r$; the first run has $k$ choices of letter and every later run $k - 1$. With $g[0] = 1$ and $g[m] = (k - 1) \sum_{j = 1}^{\min(r, m)} g[m - j]$ for the later runs, the answer is $k \sum_{j = 1}^{\min(r, n)} g[n - j]$, a sum of parts (Section 4.3) with a weight per part.

### Sums of parts with "at least one" (Theorem 3.5.3)

<Code code={compositions} lang="cpp" title="compositions/solution.cpp" />

**How it works.** `countSums` counts the sums of $n$ that use only the given parts: for ordered sums the totals are the outer loop and each total is split by its last part; for unordered sums the parts are the outer loop (Theorem 3.3.3). The sums with at least one part $\ge d$ are all sums minus the sums that use only parts below $d$ (Theorem 3.5.3); `+ MOD` keeps the difference non-negative.

**C++ details explained.** `t` and `n` are global so that the helper can read them; `const vector<int>&` passes the list without copying it. `small` collects the parts below $d$, possibly none: then `countSums` returns 0 for $n \ge 1$, and the answer is the count of all sums.

**Alternative formulation.** Add a flag to the state: $f[m][0]$ counts sums of $m$ with no part $\ge d$ so far and $f[m][1]$ those with one. A part $\ge d$ moves any sum to flag 1. It is the method of Theorem 3.5.2 and works for conditions that are not complements of something easy, such as "exactly two parts $\ge d$".

### Shares with limits (Theorem 3.5.4)

<Code code={boundedShares} lang="cpp" title="bounded-shares/solution.cpp" />

**How it works.** After person $i$, `dp[j]` counts the ways for persons $1..i$ to take $j$ items. Person $i$ takes $t$ items with $lo_i \le t \le hi_i$, so the others took $j - t$, which runs over the range $[j - hi_i, j - lo_i]$ of the previous row. The prefix sums of the previous row give that range in $O(1)$ (Theorem 3.5.4), so each person costs $O(K)$ and the whole table $O(nK) = 10^7$.

**C++ details explained.** The prefix array is rebuilt from the previous row before `dp` is overwritten, so every range sum reads old values. `max(0, j - hi)` clips the range at 0; when `j - lo < 0` the range is empty. `(prefix[to + 1] - prefix[from] + MOD) % MOD` is the subtraction of Theorem 3.5.3.

**Tested.** Stress-tested against a brute force that tries every share by recursion, on 5000 random inputs ($n \le 6$, $K \le 12$, random limits, often $lo = 0$). There are also 5 fixed tests: the example of Section 4.4 (5), two people with at most one item each and $K = 5$ (0), two people with at least two items each and $K = 4$ (1), 100 people with random limits and $K = 10^5$ (from a separate program), and 100 people without limits and $K = 10^5$ (checked against $\binom{10^5 + 99}{99}$, stars and bars).

**Alternative formulation.** Without lower limits ($lo_i = 0$), compare $\text{dp}[i][j]$ with $\text{dp}[i][j - 1]$: their ranges of the previous row, $[j - hi_i, j]$ and $[j - 1 - hi_i, j - 1]$, differ by one entry at each end, so $\text{dp}[i][j] = \text{dp}[i][j-1] + \text{dp}[i-1][j] - \text{dp}[i-1][j - hi_i - 1]$ (the last term only when $j - hi_i - 1 \ge 0$). It is the same idea without a separate prefix array.

## 7. Bug catalogue

Each bug below was made by changing a template from Section 6. The first five were caught by `scripts/stress.sh`, and the input shown is a short failing input (the shortest among the first 2000 random seeds, or a hand-made one of the same size). The last two only appear once the counts pass the modulus, which small random inputs never reach; they were caught by the large fixed tests, which is why every template has some.

### Bug: a blocked cell keeps the count from above

Template: paths in a grid.

```cpp
if (g[r][c] == '#') {
    continue;
}
```

Input: the grid of Section 4.1 (`3 4`, then `....`, `.#..`, `....`).

Wrong output `7`, correct output `4`. With one row of counts, `ways[c]` still holds the count of the cell above when a blocked cell is skipped, so the paths "through" the blocked cell are passed on to its right and below.

**Fix:** set `ways[c] = 0` for a blocked cell. With a single row, every cell must be written in every row, because the old value belongs to a different cell.

### Bug: the start cell checked before the block

Template: paths in a grid.

```cpp
if (r == 0 && c == 0) {
    ways[c] = 1;
} else if (g[r][c] == '#') {
    ways[c] = 0;
}
```

Input: `1 1`, then `#`.

Wrong output `1`, correct output `0`. The base case is written before the block is checked, so a blocked start still has one path.

**Fix:** check the block first, or set the base case only for a free start. Test with a blocked start and a blocked end.

### Bug: a new run with any letter

Template: strings with limited runs.

```cpp
next[1] = total * k % MOD;
```

Input: `2 2 5` (length 2, two letters, runs up to 5).

Wrong output `6`, correct output `4`. Repeating the last letter is counted twice: once as "the run grows" (state 2) and once as "a new run starts" (state 1). The new state is no longer determined by the string, and Theorem 3.5.2, note 1, fails.

**Fix:** a new run needs a letter different from the last one, $k - 1$ choices. Check that each object reaches exactly one state: write down the state of a short string by hand.

### Bug: unordered sums with the totals outside

Template: sums of parts.

```cpp
if (t <= 1) {   // the ordered loops used for both modes
```

Input: `1 5 2 2`, then the parts `2 3` (unordered).

Wrong output `2`, correct output `1`. With the totals outside, 2 + 3 and 3 + 2 are counted separately, but in the unordered mode they are the same sum.

**Fix:** for unordered sums the parts are the outer loop (Theorem 3.3.3). Test with a total that has two orders of the same parts.

### Bug: the range misses its last entry

Template: shares with limits.

```cpp
dp[j] = (to < 0) ? 0 : (prefix[to] - prefix[from] + MOD) % MOD;
```

Input: the example of Section 4.4 (`3 3`, then `0 2`, `0 1`, `0 2`).

Wrong output `1`, correct output `5`. `prefix[x]` is the sum of the first `x` entries, so the range `from..to` is `prefix[to + 1] − prefix[from]`; without the `+ 1` the entry `to`, here the share with the smallest amount for the current person, is lost.

**Fix:** with $P[0] = 0$, the sum of entries $l..r$ is $P[r + 1] - P[l]$ (Theorem 3.5.4). Check one range by hand on a row of three entries.

### Bug: no remainder after an addition

Template: paths in a grid.

```cpp
ways[c] = ways[c] + ways[c - 1];
```

Input: an empty $18 \times 18$ grid.

Wrong output `2333606220`, correct output `333606206`. There are $\binom{34}{17} = 2333606220$ paths, more than $10^9 + 7$; the program prints the true count instead of its remainder. On larger grids the counts overflow `long long` and the output is garbage.

**Fix:** reduce after every addition (Theorem 3.5.1). Test with an input whose answer is larger than the modulus: a large empty grid.

### Bug: a negative difference

Template: sums of parts.

```cpp
cout << (countSums(parts) - countSums(small)) % MOD << "\n";
```

Input: `0 38 3 3`, then the parts `1 2 3` (ordered sums of 38 with at least one 3).

Wrong output `-16926651`, correct output `983073356`. Both counts are remainders; the remainder of all sums (46319335) happens to be smaller than the remainder of the sums without 3 (63245986), although the true counts are in the opposite order. In C++ `%` of a negative number is negative.

**Fix:** `(A - B + MOD) % MOD` (Theorem 3.5.3, note 2). Any subtraction of remainders needs it, even when the true difference is positive.

## 8. Worked examples

### Example 1: Grid Paths I

[CSES 1638: Grid Paths I](https://cses.fi/problemset/task/1638) (CSES is the Code Submission Evaluation System problem set). In short: count the paths from the upper-left to the lower-right square of an $n \times n$ grid ($n \le 1000$) that move only right or down and avoid the traps, modulo $10^9 + 7$.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$n^2 = 10^6$ cells, so $O(n^2)$ is instant. The number of paths can have about 600 digits, so it must be kept as a remainder.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Listing the paths by backtracking (topic 3.1): there are up to $\binom{1998}{999}$ of them.
- One binomial coefficient (topic 2.3): correct only without traps.
- Inclusion–exclusion over the traps (topic 2.3): up to $10^6$ traps, far too many subsets.
- The sum over the last step (Theorem 3.5.1): the grid-paths card.

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The card needs moves that never lead back. Right and down moves increase the row or the column, so reading order is an acyclic order. Every path that ends at a free cell other than the start arrives from exactly one of two cells: the one above or the one to the left. Decision: $\text{ways}[r][c] = \text{ways}[r-1][c] + \text{ways}[r][c-1]$ modulo $10^9 + 7$, with 0 on traps.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

Apply Theorem 3.5.1 with the cells as states. A path to $(r, c)$ with its last move removed is a path to the cell above or to the left, depending on that move; different paths with the same last move stay different. So the paths to $(r, c)$ are counted once each, as the sum of the two counts. A trap is never entered, so its count is 0 and it contributes nothing. Cells outside the grid count 0, which the border row and column provide. Each cell is filled after the two cells it reads.

#### Step 6: Alternative approach

The one-row version of Section 6 uses $O(n)$ memory. Another view: count the paths from the end backwards, $\text{back}[r][c] = \text{back}[r+1][c] + \text{back}[r][c+1]$; the answer is $\text{back}$ at the start. Counting from both ends gives, for every cell, the number of paths through it, $\text{ways} \cdot \text{back}$, which answers questions such as "which single trap would block the most paths".

### Example 2: Leaping Tak

[AtCoder Beginner Contest (ABC) 179, problem D: Leaping Tak](https://atcoder.jp/contests/abc179/tasks/abc179_d). In short: cells $1..N$ ($N \le 2 \cdot 10^5$); from cell $i$ you may jump to $i + d$ for any $d$ in the union of $K \le 10$ disjoint segments $[L, R]$, never past cell $N$. Count the ways to go from cell 1 to cell $N$, modulo $998244353$.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$N = 2 \cdot 10^5$. The allowed jump lengths can number up to $N$, so trying every jump from every cell is $O(N^2) = 4 \cdot 10^{10}$: too slow. $O(N \cdot K) = 2 \cdot 10^6$ is the target.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- The sum over the last jump (Theorem 3.5.1): $\text{ways}[i] = \sum_{d \in S} \text{ways}[i - d]$, correct but $O(N \cdot |S|)$.
- Counting ordered sums of allowed parts (Section 4.3): the same table, the same cost.
- Prefix sums of the table (Theorem 3.5.4): the range-sum-transition card.

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The card needs predecessors that form ranges. For one segment $[L, R]$, the cells from which a jump of that segment reaches $i$ are $i - R, \dots, i - L$, a contiguous range of earlier cells. There are $K \le 10$ segments, so $\text{ways}[i]$ is a sum of at most 10 ranges of earlier entries. The table is filled from left to right, so every entry in those ranges is final. Decision: one table with growing prefix sums, $O(NK)$.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

A way to reach cell $i > 1$ is a sequence of jumps; its last jump has a length $d$ in exactly one segment (they are disjoint) and starts at $i - d \ge 1$. Removing it gives a way to reach $i - d$, one-to-one (Theorem 3.5.1). So $\text{ways}[i] = \sum_{\text{segments}} \sum_{j = \max(1, i - R)}^{i - L} \text{ways}[j]$, an empty range when $i - L < 1$. With $P[x] = \text{ways}[1] + \dots + \text{ways}[x - 1]$, each range is $P[\text{to} + 1] - P[\text{from}]$ (Theorem 3.5.4, note 3: the prefix sums grow as the row is filled). Disjointness of the segments matters: overlapping segments would count a jump length twice.

Push instead of pull: when $\text{ways}[i]$ is final, it should be added to every cell in $[i + L, i + R]$ for each segment. Doing that with a difference array (topic 1.2), add at $i + L$ and subtract at $i + R + 1$, and a running sum, gives the same $O(NK)$ algorithm seen from the other side.
