## 1. Why this topic exists

Here is a small problem. A weather station wrote down the temperature on $n = 2 \cdot 10^5$ days. You get $q = 2 \cdot 10^5$ questions, and each gives two days $l$ and $r$ and asks: "what was the lowest temperature from day $l$ to day $r$?" The readings never change between questions.

The direct way scans the days from $l$ to $r$. A question about the whole record costs $2 \cdot 10^5$ steps, and $q$ such questions cost up to $4 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps. At about $10^8$ simple steps per second that is about 400 seconds, far over a typical time limit of 1 to 2 seconds. The prefix sums of [topic 1.2](../../phase-1/1-2-prefix-sums/) do not help: a sum can be undone by subtracting a smaller prefix, but a minimum cannot. Writing down the answer to every pair $(l, r)$ in advance needs about $n^2 / 2 = 2 \cdot 10^{10}$ numbers.

The key idea: store the answers only for *blocks whose length is a power of two*: for every start $i$, the minimum of 1 day, of 2 days, of 4 days, of 8 days, and so on. That is about $\log_2 n \approx 18$ numbers per start, so $18 \cdot 2 \cdot 10^5 = 3.6 \cdot 10^6$ numbers in all, and each is the smaller of two numbers that are already in the table. Now take any range of $\ell$ days. The largest power of two that fits is $2^k \le \ell$, and two blocks of that length, one starting at $l$ and one ending at $r$, together cover the whole range. They may overlap, and for a minimum an overlap does no harm: the smaller of "the lowest in the first block" and "the lowest in the second block" is the lowest in the range. Each question is two lookups. This table is the **sparse table**, and it works for every operation that does not mind being counted twice: the minimum, the maximum, the greatest common divisor, the bitwise AND and the bitwise OR.

## 3. Definitions

- A **range query** asks for a value computed from the elements $a_l, a_{l+1}, \dots, a_r$ of an array $a$. This page numbers the positions from 0 to $n - 1$ and writes $[l, r]$ for the positions from $l$ to $r$ *including both ends*. A **range minimum query** (**RMQ**) asks for $\min(a_l, \dots, a_r)$. Example: for $a = [5, 2, 4, 7, 1, 3, 6]$ the RMQ on $[1, 3]$ is $\min(2, 4, 7) = 2$.
- An **operation** $\oplus$ combines two values into one. This lesson uses min, max, the greatest common divisor (GCD, [topic 0.4](../../phase-0/0-4-elementary-math/)), the bitwise AND (`&`) and the bitwise OR (`|`) ([topic 1.7](../../phase-1/1-7-bits/)), and the sum for contrast. The **range aggregate** of $\oplus$ over $[l, r]$ is $a_l \oplus a_{l+1} \oplus \dots \oplus a_r$.
- An operation is **associative** if $(x \oplus y) \oplus z = x \oplus (y \oplus z)$ (so brackets are not needed), and **commutative** if $x \oplus y = y \oplus x$. All the operations above are both.
- An operation is **idempotent** if $x \oplus x = x$ for every $x$. The minimum is: $\min(x, x) = x$. So are max, GCD, AND and OR. The sum is not ($x + x = 2x$), and neither is XOR ($x \oplus x = 0$).
- A **block** of **level** $j$ is a group of $2^j$ consecutive positions $[i, i + 2^j - 1]$. The **sparse table** $\text{st}$ has one row for every level $j$ with $2^j \le n$; the entry $\text{st}[j][i]$ is the range aggregate over the block of level $j$ that starts at $i$. Level 0 is the array itself.
- $\lfloor \log_2 \ell \rfloor$ is the largest $k$ with $2^k \le \ell$ (the floor of the base-2 logarithm). Example: for $\ell = 5$ it is 2, because $4 \le 5 < 8$. The code stores these values in a small array `lg` with $\text{lg}[1] = 0$ and $\text{lg}[\ell] = \text{lg}[\lfloor \ell / 2 \rfloor] + 1$.
- In the code an operation is passed around as `function<int(int, int)>`: a value that holds any function taking two `int`s and returning an `int`, for example the lambda `[](int x, int y) { return min(x, y); }` ([topic 0.1](../../phase-0/0-1-cpp-for-cp/) and the lambdas of the graph lessons). The code units number positions from 0, as this page does.

## 4. Theory and proofs

### 4.1 Blocks of length $2^j$

**Theorem 5.1.1 (the table of blocks).** Let $a_0, \dots, a_{n-1}$ be an array. Put $\text{st}[0][i] = a_i$, and for $j \ge 1$ with $2^j \le n$ and $0 \le i \le n - 2^j$,
$$\text{st}[j][i] = \min\bigl(\text{st}[j-1][i],\ \text{st}[j-1][i + 2^{j-1}]\bigr).$$
Then (a) $\text{st}[j][i] = \min(a_i, \dots, a_{i + 2^j - 1})$; (b) the table has $\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1$ levels and at most $n(\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1)$ entries, and building it takes time proportional to that number.

:::tip[In plain words]
The minimum of a block of 8 is the smaller of the minimums of its two halves of 4. So each row of the table is made from the row before it, with one comparison per entry, and there are only about $\log_2 n$ rows.
:::

**A tiny instance.** For $a = [5, 2, 4, 7, 1, 3, 6]$ ($n = 7$): level 0 is the array. Level 1 has the minimum of every pair: $[2, 2, 4, 1, 1, 3]$. Level 2 combines the pairs $i$ and $i + 2$: $\min(2, 4) = 2$, $\min(2, 1) = 1$, $\min(4, 1) = 1$, $\min(1, 3) = 1$, so $[2, 1, 1, 1]$. Level 3 would need $2^3 = 8 > 7$ elements, so there are 3 levels. The entry $\text{st}[2][1] = 1$ is the minimum of $2, 4, 7, 1$ (positions 1 to 4).

**Proof.**
1. *Claim (a) by induction on $j$.* For $j = 0$ the block is the single position $i$, and its minimum is $a_i = \text{st}[0][i]$.
2. Let $j \ge 1$ and assume (a) for level $j - 1$. The block $[i, i + 2^j - 1]$ is the first half $[i, i + 2^{j-1} - 1]$ followed by the second half $[i + 2^{j-1}, i + 2^j - 1]$; both have $2^{j-1}$ positions.
3. Both halves are inside the array and both entries exist: from $i \le n - 2^j$ we get $i + 2^{j-1} \le n - 2^{j-1}$, which is the last allowed start at level $j - 1$.
4. By the assumption, $\text{st}[j-1][i]$ is the minimum of the first half and $\text{st}[j-1][i + 2^{j-1}]$ is the minimum of the second half. The minimum of a block is the smaller of the minimums of two parts that together make the block, so $\text{st}[j][i]$ is the minimum of the block. This proves (a).
5. *Claim (b).* Level $j$ exists exactly when $2^j \le n$, that is $j \le \lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor$, which gives $\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1$ levels. Level $j$ has $n - 2^j + 1 \le n$ entries and each takes one comparison, so the total number of entries and the time are at most $n(\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1)$. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The two halves are next to each other and do not overlap, so the same recipe works for any associative operation (a sum, for instance, would be built the same way).
2. Only levels with $2^j \le n$ are built, and a level has $n - 2^j + 1$ entries, not $n$.
3. Memory: for $n = 2 \cdot 10^5$ there are 18 levels, at most $3.6 \cdot 10^6$ numbers, about 14 MB of `int`.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="5.1/thm-1" />

The first example is the tiny instance: the entry 1 at level 2, position 1, is step 4 of the proof (the minimum of the two halves 2, 4 and 7, 1). The second has all values equal, and the third has a single element, which has only level 0. The check compares every entry with the minimum of its block on every array of length 1 to 6 over $\{1, 2, 3\}$.

### 4.2 Two blocks cover any range

**Theorem 5.1.2 (the query).** Let $\oplus$ be an associative, commutative and idempotent operation (min, max, GCD, AND or OR), and let the table be built as in Theorem 5.1.1 with $\oplus$ in place of min. For a range $[l, r]$ of length $\ell = r - l + 1$ let $k = \lfloor \log_2 \ell \rfloor$. Then
$$a_l \oplus \dots \oplus a_r = \text{st}[k][l] \oplus \text{st}[k][r - 2^k + 1].$$
After $O(n \log n)$ preparation each query takes $O(1)$ time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Take the biggest power of two that fits in the range. One block of that length starts at the left end and another ends at the right end. Together they cover the range, and if they overlap, no harm is done as long as counting a value twice changes nothing.
:::

**A tiny instance.** The GCD of $a = [12, 18, 8, 24, 6]$ over $[0, 2]$: the length is 3, so $k = 1$. The blocks are $[0, 1]$ and $[1, 2]$ (they overlap at position 1), with $\gcd(12, 18) = 6$ and $\gcd(18, 8) = 2$. The answer is $\gcd(6, 2) = 2$, and indeed $\gcd(12, 18, 8) = 2$. With a **sum** the same rule fails: for $[1, 2, 3]$ the blocks give $(1 + 2) + (2 + 3) = 8$, while the sum is 6, because the middle element was added twice.

**Proof.**
1. Since $\ell \ge 1$ there is a unique $k$ with $2^k \le \ell < 2^{k+1}$. Let $A = [l, l + 2^k - 1]$ and $B = [r - 2^k + 1, r]$. Both have $2^k$ positions, and both lie inside $[l, r]$ because $2^k \le \ell$. So both entries exist, and by Theorem 5.1.1(a) (its proof used only that the operation is associative) they hold the aggregates over $A$ and over $B$.
2. Every position $p \in [l, r]$ lies in $A$ or in $B$. If $p$ is not in $A$, then $p \ge l + 2^k$. And $l + 2^k \ge r - 2^k + 1$, because that says $2^{k+1} \ge r - l + 1 = \ell$, which holds as $\ell < 2^{k+1}$. So $p \ge r - 2^k + 1$ and $p \le r$, which means $p \in B$.
3. So the right side of the formula combines every value of $[l, r]$ at least once, and the values at positions in both blocks twice; no value outside $[l, r]$ is used.
4. Because $\oplus$ is associative and commutative, the result does not depend on the order or the brackets, so it is the combination of the values with these multiplicities. Because $\oplus$ is idempotent, $x \oplus x = x$, so a value that appears twice can be written once. The result is therefore $a_l \oplus \dots \oplus a_r$.
5. Computing $k$ is one lookup in `lg`, and the query makes two table lookups and one $\oplus$, so it takes $O(1)$ time. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The operation is idempotent. The proof breaks at step 4 for a sum, XOR, a count or a product (see the example), and then the table cannot be used with overlapping blocks.
2. The array does not change after the table is built; one changed value can change about $n$ entries of the table.
3. $k$ is the floor of the logarithm: `lg[len]` is computed once for all lengths with $\text{lg}[1] = 0$ and $\text{lg}[\ell] = \text{lg}[\lfloor \ell/2 \rfloor] + 1$.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="5.1/thm-2" />

The first example is the tiny instance (step 4: the middle element is counted twice and the GCD does not care); the second has no overlap, since the length 4 is a power of two. The third uses AND and the fourth is a range of length 1. The last line is the sum, which the proof does not cover: it gives 8 instead of 6. The check compares the table with a plain left-to-right combination of the range, for the five operations on every array of length 1 to 6 over $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$.

### 4.3 Growing a range moves the answer one way

**Theorem 5.1.3 (searching along a range).** Fix a start $l$ and let $f(r) = a_l \oplus \dots \oplus a_r$ for $r \ge l$. For $\oplus \in \{\min, \gcd, \text{AND}\}$ on non-negative numbers (positive numbers for gcd) we have $f(r + 1) \le f(r)$; for $\oplus \in \{\max, \text{OR}\}$ we have $f(r + 1) \ge f(r)$. Consequently, for any number $x$, the statement "$f(r) \le x$" (in the first case) or "$f(r) \ge x$" (in the second) is false for the smallest values of $r$ and true from some $r_0$ on. The first $r$ where it is true can be found with $O(\log n)$ range queries, which is $O(\log n)$ time with the table of Theorem 5.1.2.

:::tip[In plain words]
If you keep adding days to the right end of the range, the lowest temperature can only stay or go down; the GCD can only stay or shrink; the AND can only lose bits. So "has the minimum already dropped to $x$ or below?" starts as "no" and, once it turns "yes", stays "yes". That is exactly what binary search needs.
:::

**A tiny instance.** $a = [6, 9, 4, 3, 8, 1]$, $l = 0$, $\oplus = \min$: $f = 6, 6, 4, 3, 3, 1$ for $r = 0, \dots, 5$, which never goes up. For $x = 3$ the statement "$f(r) \le 3$" is false, false, false, true, true, true, so the first $r$ is 3. The AND of $[15, 14, 12, 8, 7]$ from $l = 0$ is $15, 14, 12, 8, 0$, and "$f(r) < 12$" first holds at $r = 3$.

**Proof.**
1. By associativity, $f(r + 1) = f(r) \oplus a_{r+1}$. Write $y = f(r)$ and $z = a_{r+1}$.
2. For the minimum, $\min(y, z) \le y$. For AND, every bit set in $y \,\&\, z$ is set in $y$, so $y \,\&\, z \le y$. For the GCD, $\gcd(y, z)$ divides $y$ and both are positive, so $\gcd(y, z) \le y$.
3. For the maximum, $\max(y, z) \ge y$. For OR, every bit of $y$ is set in $y \,|\, z$, so $y \,|\, z \ge y$.
4. So $f$ never goes up (or never goes down), and the statement "$f(r) \le x$" cannot turn from true back to false as $r$ grows (the same for "$f(r) \ge x$" in the second case).
5. Binary search keeps the invariant "the first true position is in $[\text{lo}, \text{hi}]$", where $\text{hi} = n$ stands for "there is none". For $\text{mid} = \lfloor (\text{lo} + \text{hi}) / 2 \rfloor < \text{hi}$: if the statement is true at $\text{mid}$, the first true position is at most $\text{mid}$, so set $\text{hi} = \text{mid}$; if false, it is greater than $\text{mid}$ (step 4), so set $\text{lo} = \text{mid} + 1$. The interval at least halves each time, so there are at most $\lceil \log_2 (n - l + 1) \rceil + 1$ steps, each one range query. When $\text{lo} = \text{hi}$ it is the answer. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. One of these five operations. For a sum with negative numbers, or for XOR, $f$ goes up and down, and the statement is not monotone (for $[5, -4, 1]$ the sums are $5, 1, 2$).
2. The values are non-negative (positive for GCD). A GCD of 0 is a special case that the lesson avoids.
3. The statement is a comparison of $f(r)$ with a number, in the direction in which $f$ moves.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="5.1/thm-3" />

The first two examples are the minimum case (step 5: the first true position is 3, and the threshold 0 is never reached, so the answer is $-1$). The next two are AND: from $l = 0$ the value drops below 12 at $r = 3$, and from $l = 3$ it drops below 8 at $r = 4$. The last is the GCD, which reaches 2 at $r = 2$. The check compares the search with a scan from $l$, for the five operations, every start and every threshold from 0 to 8, on every array of length 1 to 5 over $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$.

### 4.4 Only a few changes

**Theorem 5.1.4 (few different values).** Let $a_l, \dots, a_{n-1}$ be positive integers at most $A$ and $f(r) = \gcd(a_l, \dots, a_r)$. As $r$ grows from $l$ to $n - 1$, the value of $f$ changes at most $\lfloor \log_2 A \rfloor$ times, so there are at most $\lfloor \log_2 A \rfloor + 1$ **stretches** (maximal runs of consecutive $r$ with the same value). For AND and for OR on numbers below $2^B$, the value changes at most $B$ times. Using Theorem 5.1.3, the stretches for one $l$ can be listed in $O(\log A \cdot \log n)$ time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Every time the GCD changes it becomes a proper divisor, which is at most half as big. Starting at most $A \approx 10^9$, it can be halved at most about 30 times, so it has at most about 30 different values along any row. The same count works for the bits of an AND or an OR: each change loses (or gains) at least one bit.
:::

**A tiny instance.** $a = [64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1]$ and $l = 0$: the GCD is $64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1$, a change at every step: 7 stretches, and the bound is $\lfloor \log_2 64 \rfloor + 1 = 7$. For $a = [12, 18, 8, 24, 6]$ the GCD is $12, 6, 2, 2, 2$: three stretches.

**Proof.**
1. When $f(r + 1) \ne f(r)$, the new value $\gcd(f(r), a_{r+1})$ is a divisor of $f(r)$ different from $f(r)$, so it is at most $f(r) / 2$ (the largest proper divisor of $m$ is at most $m / 2$).
2. At the start $f(l) = a_l \le A$. After $c$ changes the value is at most $A / 2^c$, and it is at least 1, so $2^c \le A$ and $c \le \lfloor \log_2 A \rfloor$. The number of stretches is the number of changes plus 1.
3. For AND, a change removes at least one set bit and never adds one (proof of Theorem 5.1.3, step 2). The value starts with at most $B$ set bits, so there are at most $B$ changes. For OR a change adds at least one bit, and at most $B$ bits can be added.
4. *Listing the stretches.* Let a stretch with value $g$ start at $r$. By Theorem 5.1.3 the statement "$f(r') < g$" is monotone, and its first true position is where the next stretch starts. One search finds it. Each of the at most $\lfloor \log_2 A \rfloor + 1$ stretches costs one search of $O(\log n)$ time, which gives the time bound. $\square$

:::note[This proof needs]
1. The values are positive (for GCD) or below $2^B$ (for AND and OR); the bound is about the *range of the values*, not about $n$.
2. Only a single start $l$ is covered. For all starts the total is $n$ times the bound: $2 \cdot 10^5 \cdot 17 \cdot 18 \approx 6 \cdot 10^7$ steps.
3. The search needs the table of Theorem 5.1.2 and the search of Theorem 5.1.3.
:::

<TheoremCode unit="5.1/thm-4" />

The first example lists the three stretches of $[12, 18, 8, 24, 6]$ (step 4: each ends where the next search finds a smaller GCD). The second reaches the bound with equality (7 stretches; step 2). The third has all values equal and so a single stretch. The check compares the stretches with the GCD of every range on twenty thousand random arrays, and counts the changes of AND and OR on twenty thousand more.

## 6. Templates

Five templates. Each shows only the function that matters; the program around it reads the input. The last three use the structure `SparseTable` of Theorem 5.1.2, which takes the operation as a function, and say so. Each template is checked against a brute force that scans the ranges, on thousands of random small inputs and on fixed tests with a single element, all values equal and the largest sizes.

### Cheapest day in a range (Theorem 5.1.2 for the minimum)

<CodeSnippet unit="5.1/range-min" />

**How it works.** `st[j][i]` is the smallest value among the $2^j$ positions starting at `i`; level 0 is the array and each higher level takes the smaller of two entries of the level below (Theorem 5.1.1). `lg[len]` is the largest `k` with $2^k \le$ `len`, filled by `lg[len] = lg[len / 2] + 1`. A query takes the two blocks of length $2^k$ that start at `l` and end at `r`; they may overlap, and for a minimum that does no harm. The queries are read as 1-based positions and shifted by one before the call.

**Alternative formulation.** For the maximum, replace `min` by `max` in both places. For a window that moves right one step at a time, a deque ([topic 1.6](../../phase-1/1-6-monotonic-stack/)) needs no table.

### The tallest tower in a range, leftmost on a tie (Theorem 5.1.2)

<CodeSnippet unit="5.1/range-argmax" />

**How it works.** The table stores *positions* instead of values. `better(x, y)` picks the taller tower, and on equal heights the smaller position. This rule never calls two different positions equal, so it is a clear order, and the overlap of the two blocks cannot change the winner: Theorem 5.1.2 applies with `better` as the operation. Level 0 holds the positions $0, 1, 2, \dots$ (`iota`).

**Alternative formulation.** For the rightmost maximum change the tie rule to the larger position. To get both the value and the position, keep the position and read the value from `a`.

### Ranges whose GCD is 1 (Theorems 5.1.2 and 5.1.3)

<CodeSnippet unit="5.1/gcd-one-ranges" />

**How it works.** The function uses the structure `SparseTable` of Theorem 5.1.2 with `gcd` as the operation. For a fixed `l`, the gcd of `a[l..r]` only goes down as `r` grows, so "gcd is 1" is false and then true. The binary search finds the first `r` where it is true (`n` if never); that range and every longer one from `l` have gcd 1, which is `n - lo` ranges. The total can reach $n(n+1)/2 \approx 2 \cdot 10^{10}$, so it is a `long long`.

**Alternative formulation.** Two pointers ([topic 1.3](../../phase-1/1-3-two-pointers/)) can move `l` and `r` with the gcd of a window kept in a structure that supports removal; the table avoids that.

### How far can the AND stay above $k$? (Theorems 5.1.2 and 5.1.3)

<CodeSnippet unit="5.1/and-reach" />

**How it works.** The AND of `a[l..r]` only loses bits as `r` grows, so it never goes up: "AND is below `k`" is false and then true. The search finds the first position where it is true (`n` if none) and the answer is the position before it. If that position is `l` itself, then `a[l]` is already below `k` and the answer is $-1$. The table uses `&` as the operation.

**Alternative formulation.** Without a table, keep for every bit $b$ the position of the next element that has bit $b$ switched off; the answer for a query is then found bit by bit in $O(30)$ time. The table version is the same for every operation.

### The sum of the GCD over all ranges (Theorems 5.1.3 and 5.1.4)

<CodeSnippet unit="5.1/gcd-sum" />

**How it works.** For each `l`, the loop walks the stretches of equal gcd. At `r` the gcd is `g`; the search finds the first position `lo` where the gcd of `a[l..lo]` is below `g`, so the gcd is `g` on `r..lo-1` and contributes `g * (lo - r)`. Then `r` jumps to `lo`. A start has at most $\lfloor \log_2 \max a \rfloor + 1$ stretches (Theorem 5.1.4), about 20 for values up to $10^6$. The sum can reach $2 \cdot 10^{10} \cdot 10^6$, which needs 64 bits (`g` is cast before the multiplication).

**Alternative formulation.** Keep a list of the pairs (gcd value, how many starts have it) for the ranges ending at `r`, and update it when `r` moves right: new list = gcd of every value with `a[r]`, merged when equal, plus the single element. It needs no table; the stretches here need no list.

## 7. Bug catalogue

Each bug below was made by changing one line of a template and running the random stress test against the brute force; the input shown is the smallest one found.

### Bug: the second block is one place too far left

Template: cheapest day in a range.

```cpp
return min(st[k][l], st[k][r - (1 << k)]);              // instead of r - (1 << k) + 1
```

Input: `2 1`, then `1 5`, then the question `2 2`.

Wrong output `1`, correct output `5`. The question asks for the single position 2 (value 5). With $k = 0$ the second block should start at $r - 2^0 + 1 = r$, but the line starts it at $r - 1$, which is position 1 (value 1). A block of length $2^k$ that *ends* at $r$ starts at $r - 2^k + 1$ (Theorem 5.1.2, step 1).

**Fix:** add the $+ 1$.

### Bug: the last block of a level is never built

Template: cheapest day in a range.

```cpp
for (int i = 0; i + (1 << j) < n; i++)                  // instead of <= n
```

Input: `2 1`, then `1 5`, then the question `1 2`.

Wrong output `0`, correct output `1`. Level 1 has $n - 2^1 + 1 = 1$ entry, the block $[0, 1]$. The condition `i + 2 < 2` is false for $i = 0$, so the entry stays at its starting value 0 and the query reads it (Theorem 5.1.1, step 5: level $j$ has $n - 2^j + 1$ entries).

**Fix:** use `<=`: a block that ends at position $n - 1$ is allowed.

### Bug: the bound itself is treated as too small

Template: how far can the AND stay above $k$?

```cpp
if (t.query(l, mid) <= k) hi = mid;                     // instead of < k
```

Input: `1 1`, then `5`, then the question `1 5`.

Wrong output `-1`, correct output `1`. The task allows an AND that is *at least* $k$, and here the AND of position 1 is exactly 5. The search looks for the first position where the AND is *below* $k$, so the test must be a strict `<`. With `<=` the position 1 is called bad, the step back falls left of $l$, and the answer becomes $-1$ (Theorem 5.1.3: the statement must be exactly the complement of the condition being kept).

**Fix:** use `< k`.

### Bug: the tie rule picks the later tower

Template: the tallest tower in a range.

```cpp
return a[x] > a[y] || (a[x] == a[y] && x > y) ? x : y;  // instead of x < y
```

Input: `2 1`, then `1 1`, then the question `1 2`.

Wrong output `2`, correct output `1`. Both towers have height 1 and the task asks for the leftmost. The wrong rule prefers the larger position on a tie. (The rule must also be a *total* order so that the overlap of the two blocks cannot change the winner; both `<` and `>` are, so this bug is only about which one the task wants.)

**Fix:** on equal heights take the smaller position.

### Bug: the search interval is one too short

Template: ranges whose GCD is 1.

```cpp
int lo = l, hi = n - 1;                                 // instead of hi = n
```

Input: `1`, then `6`.

Wrong output `1`, correct output `0`. The only range is `[6]` with gcd 6. The search must be able to answer "no position works", which is the value $n$; with `hi = n - 1` the loop does not run when $l = n - 1$, so `lo` stays at $l$ and `n - lo` counts a range that does not have gcd 1 (Theorem 5.1.3, step 5: $\text{hi} = n$ stands for "none").

**Fix:** start with `hi = n`.

### Bug: forgetting that the very first value may already be too small

Template: how far can the AND stay above $k$?

```cpp
return lo - 1;                                          // instead of: lo - 1 < l ? -1 : lo - 1
```

Input: `2 1`, then `0 0`, then the question `2 2`.

Wrong output `1`, correct output `-1`. At $l = 2$ (0-based 1) the value $0$ is already below $k = 2$, so the first bad position is $l$ itself and the answer is "none". Stepping back by one gives position 0 (printed as 1), which is *left* of $l$.

**Fix:** if the step back leaves the range (`lo - 1 < l`), print $-1$.

### Bug: the stretch is skipped after its end

Template: the sum of the GCD over all ranges.

```cpp
r = lo + 1;                                             // instead of r = lo
```

Input: `2`, then `6 4`.

Wrong output `10`, correct output `12`. From $l = 0$ the gcd is 6 on position 0 and 2 on position 1. The first stretch ends at `lo = 1`, which is the *first position of the next stretch*; jumping to `lo + 1` skips it, losing $\gcd(6, 4) = 2$ (Theorem 5.1.4, step 4).

**Fix:** continue at `lo`.

## 8. Worked examples

### Example 1: Static Range Minimum Queries

[CSES 1647: Static Range Minimum Queries](https://cses.fi/problemset/task/1647). In short: an array of $n \le 2 \cdot 10^5$ numbers and $q \le 2 \cdot 10^5$ questions $(a, b)$; for each, print the smallest number in positions $a$ to $b$ (1-based, both included).

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$n = q = 2 \cdot 10^5$. Scanning each range is up to $4 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps, about 400 seconds. A table with 18 levels has at most $3.6 \cdot 10^6$ entries, built in about as many steps, and each question is two lookups.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Scan the range for every question ([topic 0.5](../../phase-0/0-5-brute-force/)): $O(nq)$.
- Prefix minimums ([topic 1.2](../../phase-1/1-2-prefix-sums/)): they give the minimum of positions $1$ to $b$ only.
- A sparse table: the card "Range minimum, maximum, GCD, AND or OR on a fixed array (sparse table)".

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

The statement has no updates: the array is the same for all questions. The operation is the minimum, and $\min(x, x) = x$. Decision: build the table of minimums once and answer each question with two blocks.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

By Theorem 5.1.1 the table holds the minimum of every block of length $2^j$. By Theorem 5.1.2 the minimum of $[l, r]$ is the smaller of the two entries for the blocks of length $2^k$, $k = \lfloor \log_2 (r - l + 1) \rfloor$, starting at $l$ and ending at $r$, because the blocks cover the range and the minimum ignores the overlap. The 1-based input positions are shifted by one before the call.

### Example 2: Iva & Pav

[Codeforces 1878E: Iva & Pav](https://codeforces.com/problemset/problem/1878/E). In short: for each of several tests, an array of $n$ numbers (total $n$ up to $2 \cdot 10^5$) and $q$ questions $(l, k)$: find the largest $r \ge l$ such that the bitwise AND of $a_l, \dots, a_r$ is at least $k$, or print $-1$ if $a_l < k$.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

Total $n$ and $q$ up to $2 \cdot 10^5$. Computing the AND from $l$ to the right for every question is up to $4 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps. With a table the AND of any range costs $O(1)$, and a binary search over $r$ makes about 18 such queries per question: about $4 \cdot 10^6$ steps in all.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- Scan to the right for every question ([topic 0.5](../../phase-0/0-5-brute-force/)): too slow.
- Binary search on the answer ([topic 1.4](../../phase-1/1-4-binary-search/)) with an $O(n)$ check: still $O(n \log n)$ per question.
- Binary search along the range with a table: the card "Searching along a growing range (first r where the aggregate passes a bound)".

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

For a fixed $l$ the AND of $a_l..a_r$ only loses bits as $r$ grows, so it never goes up. Then "AND $< k$" is false for small $r$ and true from some $r$ on, which is the shape of Theorem 5.1.3. Decision: build an AND table; for each question find the first $r$ where the AND is below $k$ and answer the position before it.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

The AND is associative, commutative and idempotent, so the table of Theorem 5.1.2 gives the AND of any range in $O(1)$. By Theorem 5.1.3 the first $r$ with AND $< k$ is found by binary search in $O(\log n)$ queries. Every $r'$ before it has AND $\ge k$, and every $r'$ from it on has AND $< k$, so the answer is the position just before it, unless that is left of $l$, in which case $a_l < k$ and the answer is $-1$.

### Example 3: Find a Value of a Mysterious Function Closest to Target

[LeetCode 1521: Find a Value of a Mysterious Function Closest to Target](https://leetcode.com/problems/find-a-value-of-a-mysterious-function-closest-to-target/). In short: for an array `arr` of up to $10^5$ numbers up to $10^6$, the function of a range $[l, r]$ is the bitwise AND of its elements; find the smallest value of $|\text{AND} - \text{target}|$ over all ranges.

#### Step 1: Constraints → budget

$n = 10^5$ gives $n^2 / 2 = 5 \cdot 10^9$ ranges, too many even with a running AND. Values below $2^{20}$ mean that for a fixed $l$ the AND can lose at most 20 bits, so it has at most 21 different values. With a search of about 17 queries for each stretch, the work is at most $10^5 \cdot 21 \cdot 17 \approx 3.6 \cdot 10^7$.

#### Step 2: Question form → candidate tools

- All ranges with a running AND ([topic 0.5](../../phase-0/0-5-brute-force/)): $5 \cdot 10^9$ steps.
- Per-bit counting ([topic 1.7](../../phase-1/1-7-bits/)): counts the number of ranges where a bit is set, but the question asks for the closest value, not for a count.
- The stretches of an AND with a table: the card "Few different values over all starts (counting by GCD, AND or OR)".

#### Step 3: Property check → decision

For fixed $l$, every change of the AND removes at least one bit, so there are at most 21 stretches (Theorem 5.1.4). The question depends only on the *values* of the AND, not on which range gave them. Decision: for each $l$ list the stretches and take the closest value to the target.

#### Step 4: Proof for this problem

Let $V_l$ be the set of values taken by $\text{AND}(l..r)$ over $r \ge l$. The best answer is the minimum over all $l$ of the best value in $V_l$. By Theorem 5.1.4, $V_l$ has at most 21 elements, and the stretch of each value is found by one search, because the AND never goes up (Theorem 5.1.3). Each stretch is visited once, from its first position, so all of $V_l$ is found.
